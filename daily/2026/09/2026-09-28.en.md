## Business and Platforms

### Owed a billion dollars in Nvidia stock

In [a personal essay](https://colo.to/nvidia-stock-narrative.html), Eric Gullichsen describes joining Nvidia's technical advisory board in 1993, receiving 25,000 options that, by the signed letter, vested quarterly over one year, and later exercising only 15,625 shares after the CFO's 1996 letter treated the schedule as four years. Years later, after many stock splits — about a 480-fold increase — the 9,375-share difference would translate to roughly 4.5 million shares today. The author says Nvidia did not dispute the document's authenticity during a year of lawyer correspondence but argued the claim was barred by the statute of limitations — the legal deadline for bringing such claims — and the talks ended without settlement. The digest's reading is that the piece works less as a demand than as a caution about reading contracts closely and acting promptly, because clarity on paper does not enforce itself over time. Top commenters focused on that tradeoff, noting that even a plausible claim can fail at the motion-to-dismiss stage after thirty years, while others shared their own stories of missed vesting details. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49872723)

### AI companies in a race to sound the most dangerous

New Zealand satire site The Civilian publishes [a spoof news report](https://thecivilian.co.nz/2026/09/27/ai-companies-in-fierce-arms-race-to-demonstrate-their-model-is-the-most-existentially-threatening-to-humanity/) that pretends AI firms now compete to prove whose model is most capable of ending humanity, complete with invented executive quotes and a mock academic commentator. The original piece is framed as satire — the scenarios and quotations are made up — and uses that exaggeration to needle a familiar marketing move: turning warnings about risk into a proxy for power and attention. The digest finds the target is not any single disclosure, but the habit of letting alarming language substitute for verifiable safety work. Several readers on HN first flagged the satire label and then split on motive, with some arguing danger-talk helps with regulatory capture and investor storytelling, while others said concerns among researchers can be sincere and the two incentives can coexist. ## Tech and Products

### Sonnet 5.5

[Anthropic's launch post for Claude Sonnet 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5) positions the model as the everyday complement to the heavier Opus 5.5: faster, cheaper per task, and suited to bug fixes and polished documents and slides. The company reports a leap on Terminal-Bench 4.0, an agentic coding test, from about 10% to 70%, plus strong gains on knowledge-work and chart-reading evaluations, with output more than 30% faster and fewer tokens per job, at the same list price of $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. Because its cybersecurity capabilities now rival Opus, Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 ships with similar safeguards and new protections against large-scale distillation, a technique for copying model behavior. The digest's take is that the most practical shift for a general reader is token economics: doing routine work at lower reasoning effort for a fraction of the cost. The thread's main split was when to pick which model, with top comments arguing Sonnet wins at low and medium effort and Opus remains preferable for open-ended, high-judgment work, alongside practical notes on caching and fallback behavior. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49881850)

### Coding is not solved

In [a long blog essay](https://blog.alexewerlof.com/p/coding-is-not-solved), Alex Ewerlof pushes back on the claim that coding is finished and only taste remains. The source argues that most professional software lives or dies on non-functional requirements — reliability, security, scalability, and maintainability — not just on whether a feature appears to work in a demo, and that current language models, though useful, remain uneven on logic and lose accuracy as context grows, requiring harnesses and testing to stay honest. The author draws a line between high-tolerance personal tools and proofs of concept, where rough edges are acceptable, and low-tolerance systems in health care, finance, or aviation, where understanding, mandate, and accountability must stay with a human owner. The digest reads this as a call for honest tradeoffs between speed and quality rather than a rejection of AI assistance. HN reactions divided: some said with enough fuzzing, property testing, and end-to-end traces, AI-assisted code can be made more reliable than hand-written code, while others countered that reading is not the same as understanding, and that confident explanations can still be wrong. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49877988)

### Self-hosting on the dark web

Developer David Alvarez Rosa explains in [a hands-on guide](https://david.alvarezrosa.com/posts/self-hosting-on-the-dark-web/) how he made his personal site available as a Tor hidden service — an address ending in .onion that is derived from a public key and reachable only inside Tor (a volunteer-run privacy network that encrypts traffic in layers so no single relay sees both who you are and what you visit). The post walks through setting HiddenServiceDir and HiddenServicePort, reading the generated hostname file, and adding a minimal nginx server block that listens only on a local port, noting that Tor itself provides the encryption so no TLS is needed. Because static-site generators bake the base URL into links, he builds the site twice with different base URLs and deploys each to its own web root via an automated pipeline. Commenters on HN praised the clear steps but added performance advice specific to onion sites — inline assets, keep a single connection alive, and do as much rendering on the server — and flagged the trickier problems of distributing the true onion address and handling abuse. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49870295)

## Policy and Governance

### It's Time to Investigate the AI Labs

Cal Newport's [commentary](https://calnewport.com/its-time-to-investigate-the-ai-labs/) picks up recent incidents involving frontier agents and executive statements about risk and argues that public conversation is being shaped around a single, inevitable trajectory for AI. Citing his New York Times op-ed, the source calls for a congressional fact-finding effort focused on three questions: which narrow systems are actually causing problems rather than AI in the abstract, what internal safety procedures allowed risky systems to keep running after warning signs, and what role apocalyptic or messianic beliefs inside the labs play in decisions about speed and scope. The piece contends that a handful of private firms should not set the terms for how everyone feels about the technology. The thread was mixed but broadly engaged on governance: top comments agreed the first step is to disaggregate different model uses and attach controls at the environment level, while others worried that a threat-led framing could crowd out more concrete questions about liability, logging, and audit. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49883471)

## Society and Culture

### Windows 11½

[Windows 11½](https://definitelynotwindows.com/) is not a Microsoft release but an interactive parody site that pretends to be one. Built by an independent creator, it recreates a cluttered desktop — activation nudges, Microsoft 365 re-checks, Edge default-browser prompts, OneDrive storage warnings, update timers, and the return of Clippy as a subscription-aware guide — with clickable pop-ups and sound effects. The stated point is commentary on subscriptions, ads, cloud backup defaults, and AI features that repeatedly ask for attention, and the page carries explicit disclaimers and trademark notes to avoid confusion. The digest sees its appeal in recognition: many details are close enough to daily friction to feel documentary. HN readers largely responded with commiseration about attention theft on modern desktops, with several noting that even heavy customization no longer stays quiet, while others compared notes on moving to Fedora and on driver and game compatibility. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49881747)

### Pirating the Pirates

A [long feature in Mubi Notebook](https://mubi.com/en/notebook/posts/pirating-the-pirates) follows more than a decade of fan-led preservation around films like Sergio Leone's The Good, the Bad and the Ugly. The source details how official home-video releases have repeatedly altered runtime, sound mixes, and color through expanded cuts, new surround remixes, and director-approved revisions, while time and budget constraints limit how many historical versions labels can properly restore. Enthusiast communities have responded by comparing video and audio across DVDs, Blu-rays, and even LaserDiscs, stitching together editions that track more closely to original theatrical presentations — projects such as Harmy's Despecialized and Team Negative1's 4K scans — and more recently sharing material with boutique labels like Arrow for sanctioned reissues. The piece is clear that bypassing disc protections and redistributing the results carries legal risk under anti-circumvention rules, even when nothing is sold. Commenters debated the line between revision and erasure, with many arguing creators may revise while the first public version, as a piece of shared memory, should remain available, and others extending the same concern to games and software. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49880036)

### Kids turned NPR Spotify comments into a secret group chat

In [a This American Life transcript](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/897/transcript), NPR staff discover strange, fragmentary comments on a little-listened Spotify podcast episode and first assume bots. A younger colleague recognizes the pattern instead: about twenty mostly middle-school kids, largely unable to use mainstream social apps because of strict parental rules but allowed on Spotify, had been using public playlists and podcast comment threads as a clubhouse — creating playlists titled "chat here" with a single episode inside, finding each other through the public playlist listings, and talking in the comments. A 14-year-old interviewee says the group chose quiet shows, often NPR titles, precisely because they had few other commenters and would not be buried or moderated as quickly. The thread on HN was affectionate and reflective; many readers recalled their own childhood workarounds in Google Docs and other overlooked corners, and the main takeaway in top comments was that blocking alone tends to push social needs elsewhere, so curiosity about motive beats blanket removal. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49879697)

## Science and Research

### 37,500 border drawings: a map of the world as people remember it

Developer Nicolas builds [an interactive world map called The Drawn World](https://www.habibicode.org/thedrawnworld) from roughly 52,552 hand-drawn borders and coastlines collected through his geography game Borderline. Every stroke is kept and layered with opacity tied to its score, so darker lines mean many people drew in roughly the same place and the overall haze shows both the common core and the systematic drift of collective memory. The page tallies drawings by country — Brazil, China, Chile, the United States, and others lead on volume — and publishes per-country median scores and errors to compare how sharply different outlines are remembered, with no filtering for accuracy. Several readers asked whether the data could be broken down by where the drawers live to see how different regions picture the world; the author replied he does not collect that information now but would consider an opt-in, while others suggested clustering drawings to surface distinct memory patterns. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49875142)
