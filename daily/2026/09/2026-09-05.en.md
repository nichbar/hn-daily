# Today's Highlights

Start with [Nitter has more working instances than before the takedowns](https://codeberg.org/mv12star/shitter/wiki/Instances): an open-source front end that lets anyone read X without logging in, once knocked offline by blocks and takedowns, now lists more reachable instances than before the crackdown. Its lead in today’s Hacker News feed says something about the through-line across the rest of the day — when platforms tighten and automation deepens, ordinary people still want a verifiable, opt-out path to public information.

That need shows up in two directions. Bottom-up repair: repurposing rejected console chips into affordable PCs, keeping code under European jurisdiction, and giving engineers simulator time to stay close to their systems. Top-down trust tests: quietly moving national gold to where it can be traded in a crisis, using plate-reader cameras for retaliatory tracking, keeping the health risks of a ubiquitous chemical muted for decades, and testing whether collective bargaining can steady a public-knowledge institution. Bookending it are two longer-horizon signals — Europe’s first orbital launch from home soil, and a UN stocktake that treats crossing the 1.5-degree line as a matter of when, not if. The sections below follow those themes.

## Tech and Products

### [AI handles incidents, engineers lose touch with their systems](https://www.sylvainkalache.com/blog/ai-handles-incidents-engineers-lose-touch-with-their-systems)

What happens when an AI “SRE” can triage an alert, correlate a recent deploy, query observability data, and push a fix on its own? According to this essay, drawn from the author’s experience building a self-healing system at LinkedIn in 2012, mean time to recovery for routine incidents will keep falling — and hands-on intuition will fall with it. The piece leans on Lisanne Bainbridge’s 1983 “ironies of automation”: automation trims practice on the ordinary while leaving people responsible for the novel and severe. The aviation analogy does the heavy lifting — commercial pilots may go an entire career without an engine failure outside a simulator, which is why recurrent simulator checks matter — and the author describes a current experiment with Uptime Labs where engineers run a simulated e-commerce outage in Slack, coordinating through LLM-played stakeholders and learning to make calls on partial information. Watching explanations, the article argues, does not substitute for practice.

The digest’s reading is that the trade-off is not speed versus laziness, but speed versus “comprehension debt” that spikes when the rare case hits. On HN, top comments largely agreed with the warning and shared workplace snapshots of teams routing problems straight to a model and skipping patient, step-by-step debugging, with several readers noting that models often over-engineer and miss subtle concurrency or boundary conditions. The thread’s practical takeaway was to treat AI as a steerable assistant under human direction, and to keep hands-on drills and chaos experiments on the calendar.

### [Git hosting that never leaves Europe](https://pushin.eu/)

[Pushin.eu](https://pushin.eu/) frames Git hosting as critical infrastructure: all repositories live on bare-metal machines in Scaleway’s Paris data centers, stay inside EU jurisdiction, and are never used to train models. The site promises both public and private repositories, a GitHub-shaped REST API and a `pun` CLI, plus import paths that preserve history, labels, issues, and merged pull requests, or a one-way mirror for those not ready to leave GitHub. Founder Peter Ullrich, writing on HN, says he began building the service in April 2026 with his dog Bella as co-pilot, aiming for general availability in early 2027 on invite-only access and a reputation-based vouching system to tamp down low-effort, bulk contributions.

HN readers treated the pitch as a gap to fill rather than a niche — Codeberg serves free software well but limits private-use patterns, while Pushin explicitly courts both. The conversation probed how “slop” will be judged and whether single-region hosting in Paris is resilient, with the founder explaining trade-offs such as using Postgres row locks instead of an S3 write-ahead log to keep push latency down.

### [The "$60 Gaming PC" – AMD BC-250 (2025)](https://devquasar.com/hardware/the-60-gaming-pc-amd-bc-250/)

The [BC-250](https://devquasar.com/hardware/the-60-gaming-pc-amd-bc-250/) began life as a dense crypto-mining board built from binned PlayStation 5 APUs — typically six Zen 2 cores, 24 RDNA 2 compute units, and 16 GB of unified GDDR6 shared between CPU and graphics, with no DIMM slots and I/O closer to a small PC than a GPU card. A community guide describes how, with a custom BIOS that rebalances the unified-memory carve-out, modest cooling mods, and Linux (usually an Arch-based distro), the board can run games like Cyberpunk 2077 at playable frame rates. The post prices the board around $70 to $100 second-hand, with memory bandwidth near 410 GB/s also attractive for lightweight local-model inference, and links to 3D-printed case designs for a living-room build. The catch is craft: flashing firmware and case work are required, so the piece reads as a hands-on project more than a turnkey deal.

HN hands-on reports grounded the hype. One builder said the board now often lists near $150 and a complete box lands around $300 once you add a PSU, NVMe, adapters, and a printed case — still compelling next to a standard PS5 for those comfortable on Linux, and capable of a console-like experience with a Bluetooth wake switch, but not idle-friendly at roughly 80 watts and with fan noise to manage.

## Business and Platforms

### [Nitter has more working instances than before the takedowns](https://codeberg.org/mv12star/shitter/wiki/Instances)

Once the go-to way to read X without an account, [Nitter](https://codeberg.org/mv12star/shitter/wiki/Instances) went dark across many public hosts as anti-scraping measures tightened. This Codeberg wiki update reframes the story as repair: it lists working instances and redirectors, notes which hosts are rate-limited, keeps a record of former hosts, and adds guides for self-hosting, collecting session tokens, and routing around complaints. The author’s nudge is that small, access-controlled self-hosts tend to stay up longer than wide-open public nodes that attract blocks.

The HN thread split on tactics versus principles. Several top comments urged simply quitting X altogether — “stop using it, even ‘just reading an occasional post,’” in one commenter’s phrasing — and letting collective exit do the work. Others countered that read-only archiving and monitoring matter when governments and newsrooms still publish first on X, and that private, login-free access preserves a check on concentrated distribution. The common thread was that any public hub will keep playing cat and mouse, so durable access rests on distributed self-hosting and tools that rotate among hosts.

## Policy and Governance

### [Netherlands pulls gold out of the US](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-09-04/why-the-netherlands-moved-its-gold-from-us-and-canada/107111990)

Between March and August, De Nederlandsche Bank said it moved 86 tonnes of the 313 tonnes it held in the United States and Canada to London and to its domestic vault in Zeist — selling 59 tonnes in New York and buying the same amount in London, plus physically shipping about 27 tonnes. The central bank framed the shift as crisis preparedness: gold held at the Bank of England, home to the world’s largest over-the-counter gold market, meets modern trading standards and is more readily saleable, while metal in New York or Ottawa cannot be mobilized as quickly. Governor Olaf Sleijpen described gold as an “anchor of trust” for tail risk, without naming a political trigger; outside analysts quoted by [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-09-04/why-the-netherlands-moved-its-gold-from-us-and-canada/107111990) tied the reassessment to tariff threats and alliance frictions and to worries about Federal Reserve independence, alongside the broader context of war in Europe.

Commenters were less interested in geopolitics than in mechanics — why gold lives abroad at all. Many traced it to wartime dispersal and the gold-standard plumbing that left ownership on central-bank ledgers rather than in constant motion; others debated whether London truly lowers political risk if a crisis spans the Atlantic, noting the choice trades a degree of sovereign dispersal for market liquidity.

### [Flock used >100 times to track veteran who recorded traffic stop](https://reason.com/2026/09/02/wisconsin-cops-used-flock-over-100-times-to-track-a-navy-veteran-after-he-lawfully-recorded-a-traffic-stop/)

Navy veteran Napoleon Jones lawfully filmed a traffic stop from a private lot in Sussex, Wisconsin, on May 4, 2025. According to [Reason](https://reason.com/2026/09/02/wisconsin-cops-used-flock-over-100-times-to-track-a-navy-veteran-after-he-lawfully-recorded-a-traffic-stop/), a Waukesha County deputy then stopped and arrested Jones over an allegedly unreadable temporary plate, held him for five hours, and released him with no citation; an internal memo later called the stop unlawful and said identification could not have been compelled. After Jones filed a complaint, his lawyer found a Flock Safety image of his white BMW from May 30, then uncovered more than 100 Flock searches for the car in the following weeks, some by the same deputy. Jones amended his federal complaint in July to allege searches without legitimate law-enforcement purpose and retaliation for protected activity, arguing officers tried to find a crime after the fact.

The HN reaction did not dispute the core facts so much as what they imply about oversight. Some readers pointed to the paper trail — documented searches and a lawsuit — as evidence that logging and litigation can catch misuse. Others argued that without personnel consequences the deterrent is thin, and linked the case to a larger pattern: venture-backed scale making a centralized plate-reader network cheap to deploy and easy to centralize, which then amplifies even small misuses.

## Science and Research

### [Private German rocket makes history, reaches orbit from European soil](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/isar-aerospace-second-launch-norway-andoya-spaceport-spectrum-rocket)

Isar Aerospace’s [Spectrum](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/isar-aerospace-second-launch-norway-andoya-spaceport-spectrum-rocket), a 28-meter, two-stage vehicle built near Munich, lifted off from Andøya Space Center in northern Norway at 4:12 p.m. EDT on September 5 and, a little over seven minutes later, settled into an elliptical orbit with a low point near 180 kilometers and a high point near 500 kilometers. After a circularization burn it released five cubesats and a hosted experiment, according to [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/isar-aerospace-second-launch-norway-andoya-spaceport-spectrum-rocket), marking the first orbital insertion from Western European soil and a redemption after Spectrums’s March 2025 debut, when a vent-valve event and loss of attitude control brought the rocket down within a minute. Isar says its factory can produce more than thirty vehicles a year, with a payload of about 1,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit.

On HN, the mood was celebratory but comparative. Commenters welcomed independent European launch while cautioning against reading too much into a first orbital flight versus an established cadence, and they pulled in context from Ariane’s history — built in Europe, launched from French Guiana — to situate what “from Europe” now means.

### [Global warming will exceed 1.5-degree limit, UN says](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/global-warming-will-exceed-1-5-degree-limit-un-says-in-report-that-maps-path-back-below-danger-zone)

A first-of-its-kind [UN Environment Programme report covered by PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/global-warming-will-exceed-1-5-degree-limit-un-says-in-report-that-maps-path-back-below-danger-zone) acknowledges the 1.5-degree Celsius threshold agreed in Paris will be crossed in coming years — world warming is around 1.4 degrees above the mid-19th century baseline — and sketches an “overshoot” arc instead: pass the line, contain the peak, then bend the curve back down, ideally below 1.5 degrees by century’s end, by ending fossil-fuel use and adding carbon removal. The most optimistic path peaks near 1.8 degrees mid-century if ambition accelerates; current policies point toward about 2.6 degrees by 2100. The report warns the overshoot decades will bring deadlier heat, worse water and food stress, and tipping-point risks from ice sheets to the Atlantic circulation, noting that traditional tree planting can at most shave about a tenth of a degree.

HN readers largely called the headline unsurprising, given past inaction, and debated levers. One strand made a techno-economic bet — cheaper solar, wind, and storage crowding out fossil generation without asking everyone to accept austerity. Another argued that without collective rules and state action the market path will not arrive fast enough. The common ground was to limit how high the hump goes and how long it lasts.

Investor Brad Feld’s [Labor Day weekend note](https://feld.com/archives/2026/09/the-real-luxuries-in-life/) reframes luxury through a list his wife Amy sent him: time, health, a quiet mind, slow mornings, travel, rest without guilt, a good night’s sleep, calm “boring” days, meaningful conversations, home-cooked meals, people you love and people who love you back. Written as Feld approaches 61, the short post frames Labor Day as “back to school” for the year, and the list as an answer to what actually matters when one can choose.

HN did not argue the list so much as test its edges. Commenters asked whether luxury is having fewer dependencies altogether, or choosing the dependencies worth keeping — love, family, craft — and argued that the same entry reads differently across life stages and obligations, from caregiving to early career. The thread’s gentle consensus was that the items are most valuable when they are scarce, which is why mornings without an alarm feel like a luxury at all.

### [How the Disaster of "Forever Chemicals" Was Kept Secret](https://www.propublica.org/podcast/forever-chemicals-pfas-pfos-3m-secret-kris-hansen)

A [ProPublica “Paper Trail” episode](https://www.propublica.org/podcast/forever-chemicals-pfas-pfos-3m-secret-kris-hansen) tells how per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), the family called “forever chemicals” for their persistence, traveled from nonstick pans, food wrappers, cosmetics, and firefighting foam into water, soils, and blood. Reporter Sharon Lerner traces nearly a decade of reporting that 3M, the chemicals’ early manufacturer, had toxicity and blood-level data back to the 1970s yet kept producing them. The new element is a 27-year 3M environmental-lab scientist, Kris Hansen, describing how sensitive findings were contained and what it meant to carry them for years. The episode notes developmental, immune, thyroid, liver, hormonal, and cancer signals associated with PFAS and to the claim that early containment could have kept a local industrial problem from becoming planetary.

The HN conversation centered less on whether PFAS are harmful and more on how a secret of this scale held. Readers discussed information compartmentalization inside large firms and the difficulty of dissent, sharing stories of internal escalation paths that narrow rather than widen; the through-line was that without outside reporting, a widely used product line looked ordinary to the public for decades.

### [Wikimedia Foundation Workers Overwhelmingly Vote to Form Union with CWA](https://wikiworkersunited.org/announcements/2026-09-04-us-wikimedia-foundation-workers-overwhelmingly-vote-to-form-union-with-cwa/)

U.S. staff at the nonprofit that runs Wikipedia and its sister projects have voted by a wide margin to join Communications Workers of America Local 9415, according to [an announcement from Wiki Workers United](https://wikiworkersunited.org/announcements/2026-09-04-us-wikimedia-foundation-workers-overwhelmingly-vote-to-form-union-with-cwa/). The group says the unit is the foundation’s first government-recognized bargaining unit and part of a global campaign seeking jurisdiction-specific recognition, including a pending effort in the U.K. After management declined voluntary recognition following a card check, workers filed for and won an NLRB election. The statement frames goals as voice and process — transparency and accountability, input on policy and planning, clearer hiring and promotion, safer dissent, mental-health support, and pay equity — and notes more than 2,000 volunteer editors publicly backed the drive.

HN commenters rehearsed a familiar Wikipedia-finance debate: annual expenses have risen substantially even as monthly readership has been roughly flat, prompting questions about whether growth has improved editing and infrastructure enough to feel it. Others argued that steady donations have outpaced costs and that a union is as much about retaining process and trust in a volunteer-staffed commons as about wages, with the outcome pointing toward a negotiated, codified path forward.
