# Today's Highlights

The story everyone stopped to watch came from Tesla's product page: the new [Roadster](https://www.tesla.com/roadster) doesn't just accelerate hard, it lifts off the ground. Compressed-gas thrusters push the car into the air for a few seconds of controlled maneuvering before it lands and keeps driving. Whether you read that as a genuine engineering milestone or a very expensive stunt, it worked as attention: the whole internet showed up to argue about what they just saw.

The rest of the day followed a single theme: old certainties being poked, new rules still being written. Anthropic researchers used an internal AI model to settle a 25-year question about whether the NSA left a backdoor in a widely used cryptographic standard. The international body that custodies world time voted to retire the leap second. Meta slipped language into its commercial terms that legal experts say opens the door to scraping news sites. On the lighter side, a rescue crow kept sending thank-you gifts, a blogger made the case for pixel-perfect retro rendering, and a 500-hour puzzle game divided its players. Let's get into it.

## Tech and Products

### Tesla's Roadster actually flies

[Tesla laid out](https://www.tesla.com/roadster) the full spec for the new Roadster across three configurations: a base Performance version, a Thrust version with cold-gas thrusters (they vent compressed gas rather than burning fuel; zero to 60 mph in under a second, per the company), and an invite-only Rocket version that can maneuver through the air. A multi-mode fan and ground-seal system generate enough downforce to hold the car upside down while stationary, and the flying version carries a disclaimer that it is not approved for public roads. The project was first teased in 2017, and reservations are now open.

The thread spent much of its time debating whether brief levitation counts as flying at all, with one widely liked quip calling it "falling with style." Beyond the wordplay, commenters focused on practicalities: the crash risk of a high-pressure tank, danger to pedestrians, and whether the design could ever clear European pedestrian-safety rules. Others argued that an economically irrational halo car is exactly the marketing a struggling brand needs, while one lament summed up the class tension: ordinary buyers are still waiting for a $25,000 EV, and the rich got a flying toy first.

### In praise of software rendering

A retro-gaming blogger explains [why he still prefers software rendering](https://www.jamesfmackenzie.com/2026/10/13/i-love-software-rendering/): when the CPU draws every pixel, textures stay crisp squares, and the color banding of 256-color palettes gives games a gritty texture that modern hardware smoothing washes away. In the 1990s you either ran these games at low resolution in software or bought a 3D accelerator; today a fast machine can push the original DOS software renderers to resolutions nobody could use back then, including a locked 60 frames per second at 800×600 on a humble Dell OptiPlex. The author is honest that it comes down to taste — Tomb Raider's underwater sections genuinely look better accelerated.

Most of the thread read like a club of enthusiasts comparing notes, with several noting that the blur they remember from 1997-era 3D cards was bilinear filtering, an early smoothing technique that has aged badly. A few comments got technical: Build-engine games at high resolutions can overwhelm even a fast quad-core Xeon. A smaller contingent warned against hallowing nostalgia — modern throwbacks like Ion Fury ship with software renderers mostly for engineering compatibility.

### Sill puts any Mac window on your iPhone or iPad

[Sill](https://github.com/Saffsanity/sill) is a free, open-source tool that streams any single Mac window — or the whole desktop — to an iPhone or iPad in real time, with touch, trackpad, keyboard, and Apple Pencil input flowing back. The Mac side encodes video in hardware and sends it over an encrypted connection straight to the device: no account, no server in between. Connections work over the same network, a USB cable, direct wireless, or your own VPN for remote access. A nice detail: when the link falls behind, Sill drops frames rather than queueing them, so the picture stays current.

Commenters praised the restraint of a no-server, no-account design as a genuine open alternative to Apple's own continuity features. Several readers flagged the permissions involved — screen recording and accessibility access on the Mac — and suggested auditing the code if privacy matters to you. The developer answered questions about latency and comparisons with existing options directly in the thread.

## Business and Platforms

### Waymo is coming to Toronto

[Waymo announced](https://waymo.com/blog/2026/10/waymo-in-toronto/) its first move into Canada: a fully autonomous ride-hailing service in Toronto, with the first riders expected in 2028. Vehicles will first drive manually to build high-definition maps, then test with trained safety specialists aboard. The company is partnering with Toronto-based Element Fleet Management on operations and says the effort will eventually create hundreds of roles in maintenance, validation driving, and customer service; among its existing ties to Canada are the Canada Pension Plan Investment Board as an investor and Magna International as a manufacturing partner. Waymo cited data showing 95% fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes than human drivers in the cities where it operates.

The thread's dominant question was winter. Snow, ice, and lane markings erased by plows are a real test for the perception stack, and commenters quickly nominated Montreal's freezing rain as the hardest case on the continent. The debate then wandered to Cairo, Mumbai, and Lagos, splitting in two: some argued unstructured traffic may actually be easier for a machine to model than rule-bound roads, while others pointed out that in deep snow even human drivers are guessing.

## Policy and Governance

### Meta's new terms reach for your website

Under [Meta's updated commercial terms](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/metas-new-commercial-terms-will-allow-the-scraping-of-news-sites-legal-experts-say/), effective October 30, business-account users authorize Meta to use scrapers, bots, and crawlers to access the domains associated with their products and services — and to use what it collects to "provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain" its products. The terms also allow Meta to create "test credentials." Three legal experts consulted by Nieman Lab say the language could support scraping news sites for AI training, with no real opt-out: disagree, and the only choice is to stop using business accounts. Meta responded that crawling rules were previously scattered across various terms and this consolidates them, adding that content owners can still manage access via robots.txt.

The core objection in the thread was licensing: publishers cannot grant Meta rights they never held, since a lot of news content is itself licensed from third parties on non-transferable terms. Some readers read the move as a litigation hedge rather than a legal shield, and one popular suggestion was to register copyrights quickly in order to claim statutory damages. A darker consensus held that the most unsettling corporate innovation usually happens in the legal department.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50111852)

### The leap second is finally being retired

The international body that maintains Coordinated Universal Time [voted to stop inserting leap seconds](https://www.bipm.org/en/cgpm-2026/resolution-5) starting May 20, 2027, allowing the gap between atomic time and Earth's rotation to grow to as much as an hour rather than stepping the clock. The resolution cites the risk of a negative leap second — never used, and essentially untested across global infrastructure — with preparation costs compared to the year-2000 bug, the difficulty of updating IoT devices, and the timing inconsistencies created when individual vendors smooth time their own way. Taken with the decision not to add one this December, the 27 leap seconds added so far look like the last.

Top comments catalogued the sins of the leap second: Unix timestamps repeat and become ambiguous inside the extra second, the "23:59:60" representation breaks parsers, and the interval between two timestamps can become impossible to compute exactly. One commenter booked a one-second annual holiday on their calendar. A more careful voice added that wall-clock time will stay unpredictable thanks to timezone rules regardless — what becomes predictable is the relationship between POSIX time (the raw second count computers keep) and actually elapsed time.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50105777)

### eBay tightens radio gear listings, exempts ham-only equipment

New FCC rules that took effect October 13 make online marketplaces jointly responsible for unauthorized radio-transmitting equipment: devices that require FCC certification must carry their FCC ID in the listing. eBay's first notices to sellers were broad enough to spook amateur-radio operators, who feared even ham-band-only gear would need certification. After [the ARRL, the national amateur-radio association, asked for clarification](https://www.arrl.org/arrlletterissue?issue=2026-10-15#eBay-Restricts-Listings-of-Radio-Equipment_-But-Exempts-Ham-Band-Only-Gear), eBay confirmed that radios operating only on amateur frequencies need no FCC equipment authorization and are exempt, while two-way radios that reach outside the ham bands must be certified.

The conversation drifted from policy to culture, contrasting ham radio with hacker culture. Several commenters argued the difference comes down to the medium: radio spectrum is a scarce shared resource, so one rogue transmitter can poison a band for an entire region — hence the hobby's tradition of self-policing. Others grumbled that compliance warnings follow enthusiasts into every technical corner of the internet. A historical read blamed the legal environment instead: when so much of computing security research already sits near felony territory, rebels have less to lose.

## Science and Research

### Claude cracks a 25-year crypto mystery: the NIST curve seeds were timestamps

[Anthropic's research write-up](https://www.anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins) offers an unexpectedly mundane answer to a long-running suspicion. ECDSA, the elliptic-curve signature scheme behind much of everyday internet security, was standardized by NIST in 2000 with "seed" parameters supplied by the NSA and never explained — which is why many researchers long suspected a hidden backdoor. An Anthropic employee asked an internal research model to identify how the seeds were generated without being given the story: in under half an hour, Claude concluded they were not the output of any hash function at all, but the product of lrand48, a pseudo-random number generator that shipped in 1990s C libraries, initialized with Unix timestamps from 1997 and 1998. Anthropic published reproducible code and pledged the bounty money to charity; on the evidence available, the case for a backdoor got weaker, not stronger.

Most commenters credited the sleuthing, though a persistent minority argued that an innocent generation method says nothing about the selection process, since discarded candidates can't be examined. Others countered that hunting for a specially weakened curve by brute-forcing timestamps would have required far more computing power than anyone had in 1997. A separate faction turned the story into a debate about capability gaps: frontier labs can run this kind of research on internal models, while ordinary users' similar requests often get blocked by safety filters.

## Society and Culture

### A rescued crow keeps sending thank-you gifts

[A short video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sjNpfz4UzqU) tells the story of a woman who nursed an injured crow back to health and then kept receiving "gifts" from the birds — twigs, bottle caps, and beads left for her. The clip itself is brief; the rich part is the discussion it set off. Corvids (the crow family) can recognize individual people, hold grudges, and pass information about a person through the flock, sometimes across generations, which means a rescued crow may file your name away in an entire community's memory.

The comments turned into a corvid story swap: accounts of crows shadowing a feeder across a city, magpies repurposing anti-bird spikes for their nests, and rooks solving the classic "crow and the pitcher" problem that Aesop wrote about — which researchers at Cambridge actually verified experimentally in 2009. A few voices urged restraint, noting that some crow experts discourage casual feeding.

### AI benchmarks miss 90% of how Americans work

New research from talent platform [Mercor](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/) mapped 29 major AI benchmarks onto the US Labor Department's O*NET taxonomy of occupations and tasks, and found that only 208 of 923 occupations have decent benchmark coverage. Their estimate: about 90% of American working hours go to tasks with little or no benchmark coverage at all. Coverage clusters where AI exposure and payrolls are highest — lawyers' tasks are heavily tested, while equally AI-exposed legal secretaries and court clerks are nearly invisible. The authors stress the results are provisional, but the blind spots themselves are the point: public beliefs about what AI can do are shaped heavily by which jobs anyone bothered to test.

The thread moved quickly from methodology to labor politics. "AI wants the good jobs" was one popular joke; the rebuttal was that manual work isn't protected, just out of current reach, and that robot hardware is nowhere near cheap enough to scrub toilets. A second argument concerned distribution: several commenters doubted savings from automating expensive work would ever reach consumers. A few spotted an odd silver lining — if the prestige drains from white-collar jobs, it might build a little class solidarity.

### Is a 500-hour puzzle game a masterpiece or an ordeal?

[Polygon reviewed](https://www.polygon.com/order-of-the-sinking-star-impressions/) Order of the Sinking Star, the new open-world puzzle game from Thekla, the studio led by Braid creator Jonathan Blow. The project remakes and recombines several classic Sokoban-style puzzle games (Sokoban being the genre of puzzles built around pushing blocks), with one mechanic per compass direction on a vast map, and increasingly hybrid puzzles the closer you get to the middle. After a month of play, the reviewer still couldn't decide whether it's a masterpiece or an exercise in exhaustion: the opening hours are dazzling, but the sheer scale resists firm criticism — ask again next month, he writes, and the answer might differ.

Commenters split over whether "500 hours" is a selling point or a warning sign, with fans arguing that block-puzzling shines precisely because the rules are legible and the game plays like a crossword you can set down anytime. A second thread of discussion concerned the game's remakes of independent puzzle titles, with readers asking about the standing of the original designers in the collaboration.
