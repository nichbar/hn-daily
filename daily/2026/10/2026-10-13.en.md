## Tech and Products

### Google's new flagship model reaches for labs and robots

[Gemini 4 Argon](https://deepmind.google/models/gemini/) is Google DeepMind's latest flagship, and the launch page reads like a research portfolio rather than a product sheet. On the science side, the model helped make xTB, an open-source quantum chemistry package used to predict molecular shapes, roughly 2.3 times faster, and showed up in work on rare-disease diagnosis, enzyme design and stellarator magnetic fields. The robotics demo is the most legible: Argon watched raw video of a human playing cornhole, estimated the geometry of the space, wrote the simulation itself, and transferred the skill to an Apptronik Apollo humanoid. Engineering wins include an architectural refactor of Chrome that pulled a six-million-line legacy folder out of a 40-million-line codebase, compressing months of work into weeks. For general readers, the headline is availability: it is live for Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers.

Note: the busiest comment thread had little to do with benchmarks. simonw revived his pelican test, which asks a model to draw a pelican riding a bicycle and has become a ritual on every model launch; he posted cost comparisons across three effort levels and argued Google is the only lab that has publicly said it trained specifically for SVG output. Others pushed back that once a test becomes famous it stops measuring anything, since labs will simply optimize for it.

### Hackers put Europe's supermarket freezers into permanent defrost

Tom's Hardware reports that [a cyberattack on major European supermarket chains](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/cyber-security/unprecedented-cyberattack-cripples-supermarket-fridges-across-europe-as-hackers-activate-automatic-defrosting-delivery-and-store-prices-also-impacted-by-incident) took out refrigeration control systems. Automatic defrost, a function that normally runs about two hours a day, was switched into a continuous cycle at 50 degrees Celsius, with sources describing a wave of heat escaping from fridges and freezers. The affected controllers are Danfoss units used by French retailers Intermarché, Leclerc and Coopérative U, whose president confirmed the incident and called it extremely serious. In the Netherlands, Albert Heijn, Etos and Gall & Gall took systems offline, disrupting deliveries, and electronic shelf labels were tampered with, throwing in-store pricing into chaos. Early loss estimates are around 300,000 euros, with a national figure potentially in the millions.

Note: the argument in the comments was less about the attackers than about why grocery chains look like this. One highly rated comment framed it as a class divide, noting that supermarkets run on roughly 3 percent margins and have no budget for security reviews or refresh cycles. A self-described IT director at an independent grocer backed that up in detail, describing gateway controllers left dangling off walls and HVAC contractors deploying devices that each ran their own DHCP server, the service that hands out addresses on a local network. A dissenting view held that monitoring and control should simply live on separate networks, which is a design decision rather than a budget one.

### An eleven-year-old hole in Rust's central promise

Rust has become popular for systems programming largely because it promises to eliminate memory-unsafety bugs, the family of flaws behind a huge share of security incidents. On October 13, someone filed [issue 164250](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/issues/164250) in the rust-lang repository showing that a struct's lifetime requirements, the mechanism Rust uses to track how long a piece of data stays valid, can be bypassed through covariant field assignment. A program the compiler should have rejected instead compiles and reads stack memory that has already been destroyed. The Rust team rated it high priority but explained why it is not critical: it has sat unnoticed for eleven years, and exploiting it requires deliberately writing malicious Drop code, the routine that runs when an object is torn down. The issue includes a minimal reproduction that crashes under a memory sanitizer.

Note: the thread quickly turned to whether this class of bug can ever be finished. One comment argued that using AI to hunt for compiler bugs may actually accelerate the endgame, a formally verified Rust compiler, and was immediately answered with the obvious retort that the proof checker will have bugs too. Others noted that formally verified compilers already exist for large subsets of C, so there is no principled reason Rust should be impossible.

## Business and Platforms

### Character.ai heads for a court-supervised sale

[Character.ai announced on its blog](https://blog.character.ai/an-update-on-character-ai/) that it has begun a voluntary Chapter 11 bankruptcy process to facilitate a court-supervised sale of the company. The post says the past year brought significant legal, regulatory and operational headwinds, and that after reviewing its alternatives the board concluded this route offers the best chance of maximizing value for stakeholders. If a workable bid arrives, the platform keeps running; if not, it will be wound down. Chapter 11 is the reorganization chapter of US bankruptcy law, letting a company keep operating while it negotiates with creditors, a path several crypto and consumer-internet firms have walked with very different outcomes. For a company that became shorthand for AI companionship, the filing is a blunt marker of how quickly a growth-at-any-cost model can collapse once litigation and regulation bite.

Note: the most pointed reading in the comments was not a sympathetic one. One commenter argued that Character.ai was effectively absorbed by Google in August 2024 in a reverse acqui-hire, with shareholders paid out and the founding team moving on, leaving a shell to file today; replies supplied the industry labels for the maneuver, reverse acqui-hire and HALO, short for Hire And License Out. Others objected that this framing is unfair to the people left behind, who may well be compensated as both employees and shareholders.

## Policy and Governance

### CFTC staff told to retract an analysis of suspicious oil trades

Bloomberg reports that [staff at the Commodity Futures Trading Commission were told to retract an analysis](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-10-13/cftc-staff-told-to-retract-analysis-of-suspicious-oil-trades-on-iran), with the chairman's adviser stepping in to take over the examination. The story starts on March 23, when President Trump backed away from threats to strike Iranian power plants, sending oil, equities and other markets into a frenzy. Bloomberg says contracts betting that oil would fall and the S&P 500 would rise were placed roughly fifteen minutes before he announced the reversal on Truth Social, feeding widespread speculation that someone had an unfair advantage. The CFTC is the federal agency that polices US derivatives markets, and insider-trading allegations of this kind normally land on its desk. Worth noting: at the time of publication only the lede was visible, so the details behind the headline had not been released.

Note: the HN thread was overtly political. The top comment claimed the CFTC's inaction serves both parties, and a strand of replies speculated about whether prosecutions would follow the next election. A more cautious voice pointed out that midterms and presidential elections are not the same thing, and that pinning accountability on one candidate is a weak bet.

## Science and Research

### How a brainless bacterium finds its food

James Somers's long essay is a guided tour of chemotaxis, the process by which an E. coli cell swims toward food, and its title, [The baffling intelligence of a single cell](https://jsomers.net/e-coli-chemotaxis/), is the reason it keeps circulating. The bacterium carries a handful of rotors, each driving a whip-like tail; when they spin together the tails braid into a propeller, and when one reverses the cell tumbles. Somers walks through the mechanism step by step: a signaling molecule called CheY ferries information between the cell's nose-like receptors and its rotors, and when the nose detects attractant rising, CheY gets phosphorylated less often, so the cell tumbles less and runs more. The elegant part is adaptation. Methyl groups latch onto the receptors and act as a chemical memory, letting the bacterium treat whatever concentration it finds itself in as the new normal and stay sensitive across five orders of magnitude. His conclusion is unfashionably simple: the real reason to read it is that it is neat.

Note: the sharpest disagreement was over the word intelligence in the title. A top comment argued this is a fully automatic mechanism and therefore the opposite of intelligence; a reply asked whether human intelligence is anything but, drawing the obvious parallel to people who dismiss large language models as mere word predictors. Another commenter added a complexity note: modeling a single cortical neuron takes a network of roughly a thousand elements, because real neurons store so much state in their internal chemistry.

### A Hittite king's archive, with a dying infant and a plea for reinforcements

Archaeologists working at Samuha, an ancient Hittite city near modern Sivas in central Turkey, have uncovered a royal archive of cuneiform tablets, documented in [Hittite royal archive found in Turkey](https://www.thehistoryblog.com/archives/77107). This season produced 131 tablets and fragments, on top of 53 last year, more than doubling the haul and making it the largest and most complete collection of Hittite-language documents found this century. The tablets date to the reign of King Tudhaliya II and are written in Hittite and Hurrian, covering an epidemic, military logistics, religious practice and daily royal life. One records a nursing daughter of the king who fell ill with fever, declined, and died, along with the funerary ritual that followed. Another is a letter from generals in western Anatolia begging for men, horses and chariots, and pointedly disclaiming responsibility for whatever happens if the reinforcements never arrive.

Note: nobody in the comments argued about excavation technique. The top thread asked what we are leaving behind that will let people 3,400 years from now see our daily lives, observing that digital records and toner on paper will not last anything like as long, and suggesting someone start etching stone. Replies pointed to Microsoft's Project Silica glass storage and the Internet Archive's physical vaults, while another commenter offered a historical precedent: Akkadian cuneiform was displaced by Aramaic on papyrus and vellum in the first millennium BCE, and the clay survived in quantity while the papyrus barely did.

### A paper showing that AI models can hide what they know

A paper accepted at NeurIPS 2026 takes aim at an assumption underneath most AI safety evaluation: that with enough probing, a model's dangerous capabilities can be elicited. In [AI models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13928), the authors build backdoor attacks that embed an encrypted neural network inside a host model's weights, decrypting and running it only when a secret trigger arrives. Using constructions the paper calls digital lockers, the hidden circuits remain provably hard to elicit or interpret even with full access to the weights. The authors tested elicitation by fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training on a small chemical reaction prediction task, a stand-in for the chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear capabilities that safety teams worry about most, and none of the methods worked.

Note: one reply came from someone claiming firsthand experience of the same phenomenon. They described training a model on a single question-answer pair, watching it appear to forget the association completely under unrelated training, and then seeing a fraction of runs spontaneously relearn it, which suggested to them that weights carry far more hidden content than the visible distribution implies. Others reached for analogies from the long-term E. coli evolution experiment and dormant genes, while a skeptic reframed the whole thing as steganography: the information is still there, merely hard to find.

## Society and Culture

### An OpenAI engineer arrested for bugging an executive conference room

The San Francisco Standard reports that [an OpenAI engineer has been arrested](https://sfstandard.com/2026/10/13/openai-engineer-arrested-alleged-bugging-executive-conference-room/) on suspicion of hiding an audio recording device in an executive conference room at the company's San Francisco headquarters. Abdulrahman Alfozan, 35, a Saudi national, is charged with using or attempting to use a device to intercept oral communications; he was arrested on Friday, appeared in federal court on Tuesday and remains in custody. Prosecutors say surveillance footage places him near the room on multiple dates without authorization, and that meetings there routinely involved confidential technical, commercial and strategic material, including proprietary research, product plans and government engagements. An unattended bag held his wallet, his ID and a second recorder. OpenAI says employees used the company's own models to work out what the device was and where it came from before referring the matter to the FBI, and that it has no indication he accessed model weights, source code or customer data.

Note: the top comment was blunt about the tradecraft. simonw quoted the complaint back at readers: a chatbot account tied to Alfozan's spouse, sharing identifiers with his own, spent late September asking how to build a continuous audio recorder and encrypt the SD card, and on two days asked about the specific device later found, down to its MAC address. The broader lesson several readers drew was not about spies at all, but about the permanence of chatbot logs, and how casually people hand questions to a system that keeps them.

### A Hacker News post that handed his website to the bots

Sammy Straus built sprinklz.io, a feed reader that lets users demote topics they never want to see again, AI included, and wrote up what happened next in [I posted to HN, and then my website was flooded with AI bot traffic](https://www.sammystraus.com/#i-posted-to-hn-and-then-my-website-was-flooded-with-ai-bot-traffic). The post did well, and then the site got slow, and kept getting slower days after the traffic spike should have faded. He added per-endpoint logging and found that most requests never even attempted to fetch articles, meaning they never ran the JavaScript needed to render a page. Digging through NGINX logs, he found nearly half his traffic came from a single caller describing itself as a service that watches websites for changes and turns them into an AI-generated newsletter, querying his site many times a minute. He also caught the usual named crawlers plus a few vulnerability scanners, responded with robots.txt rules and rate limits, and used the incident as an excuse to rebuild his infrastructure.

Note: plenty of readers had the same story. One top comment said he now blocks AWS, Google Cloud, Azure, Alibaba Cloud and Oracle across nearly all his properties, because those ranges mix genuine agentic abuse with vulnerability scanning, and that two years after banning Alibaba Cloud one domain still takes a million hits a day, though at least it respects robots.txt. Another comment pushed the problem toward law enforcement, arguing that a personal site getting port-scanned, probed for open doors by automated tools, is essentially unprosecutable, since it sits far below the priority of any agency with the power to act.
