# Today's Highlights

The day's most-discussed submission was a short appeal posted on [Nitter's homepage](https://nitter.net/): X Corp has sent two cease-and-desist letters in two months demanding that every Nitter instance be taken down permanently and the project's repository deleted — and the legal representation Nitter said it had secured turned out not to exist. Nitter is a third-party client that lets people read public posts on X without logging in, so the stakes reach past one hobby project. When the digital town square is privately owned, whoever holds the lawyers gets to decide how you read it.

Three threads ran through the rest of the day. The first was extending the life of things: LineageOS 24 keeps older phones patched, an open-source engine revives a 1997 strategy classic, and one developer paid strangers to try his instruction manual. The second was rules: New York's "Click to Cancel" subscription regulation came into force, and a new batch of internal documents surfaced in the climate-deception case against ExxonMobil. The third was the AI argument — Terence Tao on mathematics after proof scarcity, and Andrej Karpathy warning that society's shared understanding of what large language models can do is splitting apart. Let's dig in.

## Tech and Products

### LineageOS 24 keeps older phones out of the drawer

The open-source Android distribution [LineageOS 24](https://lineageos.org/Changelog-32/) is out, built on Android 17 and rebased earlier than usual. It merges Android security patches from August 2025 through October 2026, with older branches covered through November 2025; on top of that comes a new photo editor called Canvas, a redesigned updater that streams installations to cut flashing time, and refreshed gallery, camera, browser and music apps. The project also noted that it turns ten this December. For non-technical readers, the point of a release like this is simple: it postpones the moment a perfectly usable phone becomes e-waste.

Note: the thread read like a support group for long-lived hardware. Several commenters celebrated that their Pixel 4a and OnePlus 7 are still on the device list and can survive another year, while others pointed out that banking apps, not dead batteries, are what usually force a replacement. The most sobering reply came from a GrapheneOS maintainer, who argued that baseband and firmware flaws on end-of-life devices generally go unpatched — a reminder that "keeping phones alive" has real limits.

### An open-source engine rebuilds Total Annihilation for modern machines

[Nanolathe](https://nanolathe.gg/) is an MIT-licensed (freely usable, modifiable and redistributable) open-source engine that aims to run the 1997 strategy classic Total Annihilation the modern way. The site shows off a new GPU renderer that redraws terrain, units and explosions on today's graphics cards while keeping the original's physics feel; the code is public, a playable demo is available, and the team frames it as a rewrite rather than an emulator — gameplay logic rebuilt to run natively on Windows, macOS and Linux.

Note: for a general audience, this is a story about who gets to resurrect an old game when the rights holder won't. The comments turned into a reunion for classic real-time strategy fans, with the strongest recommendations going to Beyond All Reason, an open-source spiritual successor said to support matches with hundreds of players and tens of thousands of units, plus a welcoming community. Skeptics countered that it plays more like arcade StarCraft than the original, and the thread drifted into nostalgia over the 2.5D camera and debates about how marketing decides which niche games survive.

### He paid strangers to follow his README — and took the criticism

In [I paid people to try and follow my README](https://shkspr.mobi/blog/2026/10/i-paid-people-to-try-and-follow-my-readme/), a developer describes a small experiment. A README is the document that explains how to install and use a piece of software; suspecting his own blind spots, he recruited volunteers, paid each one 25 euros for an hour of video call, and asked them to share their screen and talk through every step aloud while he took notes by hand. Cost: about 150 euros. Findings: a broken demo link, readers trying to read the file inside a terminal, confusion about renaming hidden files, unexplained quoting rules, jokes that confused more than they amused, and one entire section only the author found interesting. He revised the document after every session.

Note: asked why he didn't just have a language model simulate users, the author said he wanted real people — their laughter, their frustration, their unscriptable perspective. Commenters recognized the deeper problem: notes written for yourself become unreadable within weeks, because the context lived only in your head at the time. The discussion then turned to the inherent ambiguity of language, with several readers arguing that explaining a concept with no room for misinterpretation is nearly impossible — and exactly what makes documentation hard.

## Business and Platforms

### After 12ft.io shut down, another paywall bypass appears

[WallHop](https://wallhop.io/) is a paywall-bypassing tool built by a developer after the shutdown of 12ft.io: paste a link, read the story, no signup required, with an iPhone share-sheet shortcut, a bookmarklet (a tiny tool you keep in your browser's bookmarks) and an API. Its pitch is "Read the article. Skip the wall." The author draws his own line in the responses — pay for publications you read regularly, he says; this is for the one-off article a friend sent you. The commercial interest here is the churn itself: paywall circumventers keep being reborn, which suggests the standoff between subscriptions and the free-floating habits of the open web is nowhere near settled.

Note: the thread pulled in two directions. One camp argued from reporters' livelihoods that bypassing paywalls is unethical, and that the real problem is how often micropayments and universal-subscription schemes have failed. The other held that requesting a public URL and choosing how to render it is the reader's business — and that the paywall itself is the bait-and-switch. A separate faction pointed at a shifting incentive: AI companies paying for news summaries may reshape the economics more than any bypass tool ever could.

### The RAM shortage is bringing DDR4 back from retirement

The Verge reports that [the RAM shortage is bringing back DDR4](https://www.theverge.com/games/1009140/ram-shortage-intel-amd-ddr4-comeback): with memory prices climbing worldwide, Intel and AMD are reviving a standard the industry had already retired. Gigabyte announced motherboards for upcoming Intel chips on the LGA 1700 socket — launched in 2021, dropped in 2024, and conveniently compatible with both DDR4 and the pricier DDR5 — with a launch expected in early 2027. AMD is going the same way with new DDR4-capable AM4 boards tied to the tenth-anniversary return of the Ryzen 7 5800X3D. For everyday builders, that means a cheaper upgrade path; the backdrop, per the article, is AI servers eating the supply of high-end memory and dragging consumer prices up with them.

Note: the comments split over whether suppliers are deliberately withholding supply. One camp compared it to the dot-com-era buying spree for Cisco routers nobody ever used, arguing companies simply milk a hot market while it lasts. The economics-minded countered that in a supply-constrained market bidders set the price floor, and pointed to China's CXMT launching at roughly the going rate as evidence against a cartel story. Others pushed back on the bubble analogy itself: this time the compute is actually being used, so elevated prices should persist until capacity catches up.

## Policy and Governance

### X's legal letters push Nitter into a public appeal for help

[Nitter's homepage](https://nitter.net/) now carries an October 10 update: on August 24 and again on September 8, X Corp sent cease-and-desist letters demanding a permanent takedown of Nitter instances and the project's repository. The author had previously said the project would continue with legal representation in place — an assurance he now says turned out to be untrue, and for which he apologizes. Nitter currently has no legal counsel at all, and he is publicly seeking funding and legal help. The tool lets people read public posts on X without an account, so how this fight ends shapes whether ordinary readers can browse public discussion without a platform's permission.

Note: the thread split on legality. One side compared Nitter to mirroring The New York Times and figured it had no viable future. Another argued the analogy fails because X does not own user-generated content the way a newspaper owns its articles — making the claim closer to a telecom asserting ownership over your conversations — and said a case like this deserves real representation. A further worry: X could add licensing terms for exclusive content, shifting the burden of filtering onto small developers with no reliable way to comply.

### New documents add weight to the climate case against ExxonMobil

Inside Climate News reports that [freshly released documents](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/07102026/exxonmobil-climate-deception-new-evidence/) add evidence to Massachusetts' climate-deception lawsuit against ExxonMobil. Among them is an internal 1988 memo from Exxon research executive Frank Sprow, written weeks before the United Nations created the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, its climate-science advisory body, warning that a global consensus on greenhouse-gas mitigation could bring "substantial negative impacts" on the company; the piece also publishes, for the first time, recent statements from Exxon scientists challenging the company's own biofuels and carbon-capture claims. The timing matters: the Supreme Court has just heard Exxon's and Suncor's argument that Boulder, Colorado's lawsuit should be halted, a decision that could determine whether two dozen similar claims proceed.

Note: the comment section went straight to accountability. The top wry observation was that nobody goes to jail, "because money is the best insulator there is" — which opened a debate between dissolving the company outright and prosecuting the individuals who made the decisions. Most argued that corporate "death penalty" talk punishes ordinary employees, while individual accountability is fairer, if notoriously hard to prove. A quieter point: these files only exist because a government lawsuit forced the company to hand them over during discovery — the pretrial phase in which internal records must be produced — suggesting litigation remains the one channel that puts internal archives on the public record.

### New York's Click to Cancel rule takes effect

New York City's ["Click to Cancel" rule](https://www.nyc.gov/main/click-to-cancel) took effect on October 1, enforced by the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection — the first municipal rule of its kind in the nation. The city's page spells out the requirements: businesses must explain subscription terms clearly, disclose consumer rights, offer a cancellation process as easy as sign-up through the same channel, and stop mailing "free" items and then demanding payment. Violations carry civil penalties starting at $525, with potential refunds. Residents can file complaints about forced phone cancellations, delays, or undisclosed auto-renewals using forms available in a dozen languages. In plain terms, the rule turns "cancelling is harder than subscribing" from a dark pattern into a finable offense.

Note: the argument in the thread was about where the line sits. One commenter with a legal background worried that phrases like "as easy to use" come with no examples, leaving good-faith businesses exposed; others called that deliberate obfuscation — if you can sign up online, you can cancel online, and the rule should spread. Several readers predicted companies will route around it with annual contracts and early-termination fees, using Adobe's billing as the test case: one side said the terms are printed right under the price, the other said selling "annual, billed monthly" as a monthly plan is the misleading part.

## Science and Research

### Terence Tao: mathematics is entering an era of proof abundance

In his Caltech talk [Math 2.0](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/math-2-0-caltech-2026.pdf), Fields Medalist (mathematics' closest equivalent to a Nobel) Terence Tao lays out a frame for what AI is doing to his field. Traditional mathematics, he argues, ran on proof scarcity: solutions to open problems were hard, rare and expensive, and institutions, traditions and incentives all organized around that assumption. Proofs happen to be objectively verifiable, fully digitizable and backed by huge high-quality corpora — exactly the properties modern machine learning exploits, which is why frontier labs prioritized math over other domains. The result is a coming era of proof abundance. But AI capability is jagged: dazzling at problem-solving, weak at subjective or real-world-dependent tasks, and the press and social media overstate the progress — most frontier problems remain unsolved. His conclusion: blindly optimizing for problem-solving now harms mathematics, and "Math 2.0" must expand the frontier while decentering solving as the discipline's core purpose.

Note: the thread fixated on one analogy in the slides — whether you would take a cancer drug whose mechanism no human understands. The most upvoted reply dismissed the premise outright — "No. I'm gonna die, my man... Just needs to cure cancer, that's nearly the only requirement" — and several commenters noted that medicine already works through trials rather than full understanding, with anesthesia's mechanism still unknown. Others accused the analogy of assuming the cure already works, when validation is the actual ethical problem, and a few used it to argue that theoreticians drift from ordinary life — the debate itself illustrating Tao's point about jagged, misaligned expectations.

## Society and Culture

### Karpathy: society's shared understanding of AI capability is splitting apart

Andrej Karpathy [reposted an old thread](https://twitter.com/karpathy/status/2109361546505966046) to argue that the gap in shared understanding of large language models is widening — no longer two groups talking past each other, but a steep funnel. His rough arithmetic: around 6 billion people have barely touched an LLM; 1 to 2 billion use free chat products casually, as a better search engine or writing aid; some 20 million professionals in math and code watch agents complete weeks-long projects in a prompt; and perhaps 5,000 people have internal access to frontier systems running swarms of agents on multi-week software projects, machine-speed cyber operations, and new science. Ordinary users and frontline professionals are living in different decades of the same technology.

Note: the community was not fully convinced. One heavily liked reply listed the unchanged realities of daily life — the same trains, more expensive housing, the same cancers — and mocked the "country of geniuses in a datacenter" as delivering nothing. Others countered that the shift is only months old and compared it to the decades it took electricity, radio and the internet to reach ordinary routines. A structural reading also appeared: factories and housing can't be refactored by writing code, so institutions and political will remain the bottleneck — a dispute that rather proves Karpathy's funnel.

### The Onion is asking readers to sell back its missing print issues

The Onion has launched an [archive-hunt page](https://theonion.com/archivehunt/) saying a batch of its print issues has mysteriously gone missing and asking readers to sell or submit copies. The page explains how to identify what you have — every issue carries a volume and issue number, printed in different spots depending on the era, with specials sometimes styled as "22-01" instead of "Volume 22 Issue 1" — then lists the years and issues it most wants, annotates sample covers from 1992 and 1997, and ends with submission instructions for anyone sitting on old bound volumes. It is written in the Onion's usual half-serious register, so whether this is a genuine archival rescue or one more joke is genuinely hard to tell.

Note: the thread's running joke asked whether the magazines were stolen or the statement was just being dramatic, with replies like "they could have lost them — doesn't need to be nefarious" and "hard to tell if the Onion is being funny". A more sober theory: early-stage publications rarely preserve their back issues, and storage budgets are the first thing cut. Several readers also caught the "270-year archive" line and dutifully checked — the Onion was founded in 1988 — which is precisely the kind of fictional patina the brand trades on.

### North Korea's map was drawn, line by line, by volunteers who never went there

[Cartographers of North Korea](https://cartographers-nk.wonyoung.so/) examines an oddity: North Korea on OpenStreetMap, the free world map, was mostly drawn by people who have never set foot in the country. The researcher wrote to the contributors and published 211 replies. The vast majority work from satellite imagery — "armchair mapping" — tracing roads, rivers and building outlines into blank spaces, each for their own reasons: a relaxing hobby, a bet on reunification someday needing maps, a principled preference for uncensored data, or cross-checking migratory bird routes. The study also estimates the top contributors' output and concludes the map is not a state-directed effort but the accumulation of scattered global volunteers.

Note: the letters themselves are the story — a Californian editor who pauses because tracing across the demilitarized zone between the two Koreas is depressing, a German recalling the errors and falsifications on Cold War maps, a Ukrainian volunteer who knows Pyongyang only from orbit. The HN thread added a ground-truth layer: commenters traded links to tourist videos now allowed mainly for Russian visitors, cautioning that the footage is likely staged along fixed routes, while a decade-long OpenStreetMap contributor said vandalism is so rare they have almost never been the first person to notice it. The researchers admit the sharpest limitation: residents can't use this beautiful dataset, because it requires the internet. For general readers the takeaway is a quiet one about digital public goods — in the blanks of a map, strangers are drawing what the people who live there are not allowed to.
