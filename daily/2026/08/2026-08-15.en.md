# Today's Highlights

The best place to start today is the story of [the Sean Byrne who doesn't exist](https://conic.al/writing/the-other-sean-byrne-doesnt-exist/): a fictitious employee invented to make a tiny Irish aircraft-parts shop look bigger, which then lodged itself in a U.S. export-control list and has spent sixteen years flagging a real man with the same name. That tale of brittle identity and sticky bureaucracy sets up the day's through-line — how systems that remember and decide at scale can help or harm, depending on how they are built. It pairs naturally with the argument in [AI isn't outthinking mathematicians](https://davidepiffer.com/p/ai-isnt-outthinking-mathematicians) that models often win not by deeper insight but by vastly larger external memory, a kind of infinite scratch paper. Around those two poles are stories about living with uncertainty: an earthquake off Flores, an [at-home test for infected ticks](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/the-first-at-home-test-for-infected-ticks-could-improve-lyme-disease-diagnosis-180989235/), and a much-hyped Alzheimer's operation, plus notes on working with AI, a [ghost in Unicode](https://www.dampfkraft.com/ghost-characters.html), and a coin-sized way to talk to a jet's computers. ## Tech and Products

This section is about memory, curves, and buses — the quiet infrastructure that decides what tools can do.

### AI's edge in math may be memory, not insight

In [AI isn't outthinking mathematicians](https://davidepiffer.com/p/ai-isnt-outthinking-mathematicians), Davide Piffer argues that when a model solves a hard math problem, part of the trick is less mysterious than it looks: human working memory is tiny, while a language model can keep the entire problem, hundreds of intermediate steps, failed attempts, and constraints in its context window. The piece reviews developmental research suggesting working memory predicts math performance even after accounting for IQ, and notes that paper and notation already act as external memory for people. Mathematics, rendered in explicit symbols, is especially well suited to an intelligence that thinks by writing. Think of two people given the same proof: one must hold it all in their head, the other gets unlimited paper and a checker — the contest changes.

The digest's reading is that the memorable comparison is not human versus machine but von Neumann versus Einstein: breadth, speed, and bookkeeping against reframing the problem itself. Top comments on Hacker News largely agreed that much of what looks like brilliance is better recall and steady bookkeeping, with originality often coming from combining two known ideas; several readers pushed back that understanding and choosing the right model under incomplete information matters more than perfect transcription, and that a longer, verifiable proof is not the same as a significant one.

### Hyperbezier curves try to make drawing feel more natural

In [The mathematical beauty of hyperbezier curves](https://linebender.org/blog/hyperbezier/), longtime type and graphics engineer Raph Levien proposes a new curve family defined by curvature as a function of arc length, a formulation that in plain terms lets a designer describe how sharply a line bends along its length. The family is presented as a candidate to complement cubic Beziers in vector design: at small angles it closely matches familiar Bezier behavior, while at larger tensions it stays smooth and can represent circles exactly, approximate hyperbolas and elastica well, and ease into sharp corners without extra wiggles. The post derives a simple closed form for the underlying equations and discusses mapping familiar handle controls to the new parameters so designers can tweak shapes intuitively. Levien notes open work on fitting, subdivision, and wiring the family into a spline.

Several readers praised the mathematical elegance and the visual quality in the interactive demos; the thread's main split was about interactivity, with some commenters noting that tight control-point moves can send the curve far outside its hull or jump between shapes, which would need careful tuning before replacing Beziers in production tools.

### A coin-sized implant that can talk to a Boeing 737's autopilot

A [WIRED report](https://www.wired.com/story/this-coin-sized-device-can-hack-a-boeing-737/) describes research from UC San Diego and Oberlin College showing that a small, sub-$100, Wi-Fi-capable device — about the size of a coin — can be slipped in under a minute into an externally accessible port on a 737 and send spoofed commands over an internal bus. The device, demonstrated on a lab rig built from purchased 737 parts and later in a Boeing test facility, can alter the flight-management computer and the display unit to change waypoints or nudge takeoff variables like weight and outside temperature while hiding the change on the pilot's screen. The researchers stress that a pilot taking manual control overrides the autopilot and that another display shows true values, but they warn that subtle diversions — even a few degrees over an ocean — could be dangerous and hard to diagnose in the moment. Boeing told WIRED it reviewed the design and believes layered protections limit real-world risk; the researchers suggest the simplest near-term fix is to remove or epoxy the exposed connector, with longer-term work on isolation and cryptographic authentication.

Top comments argued this is mostly about physical access — ground staff already have deep trust, much like a mechanic with your car's diagnostic port — while others said it highlights how civilian avionics buses long lacked authentication that military systems already use, and that publishing the work helps update threat models for ground security.

## Policy and Governance

This section looks at how names get written into lists, and how hard they are to remove.

### The other Sean Byrne doesn't exist

In [The other Sean Byrne doesn't exist](https://conic.al/writing/the-other-sean-byrne-doesnt-exist/), Dublin-based security engineer Sean Joseph Byrne traces how a common Irish name and a house in Drumcliffe, County Sligo, have trailed him for years. The U.S. Consolidated Screening List still shows a 2009 Bureau of Industry and Security Entity List entry for Sean Byrne at Cloonmull House — described as associated with the aircraft-parts firm Mac Aviation — with a note that exports are subject to a presumption of denial. Court records cited in the piece say that name was one of several invented by Mac Aviation's owners to appear larger; a superseding indictment repeatedly calls it an alias used for invoices and emails, not a separate person. The real Byrne, who has never lived in Sligo, writes that Nasdaq once flagged a share sale, DHL and SpaceX asked for passports before shipping a Starlink pole and even a hat, and Apple — after receiving his passport, driver's license, and a detailed explanation — still replied that he "fully" matched a restricted party. The essay's broader worry is that modern hiring stacks propose screening applicants at submission and sharing flags across employers — exactly the kind of early, opaque, name-only check that already misfires on him.

The thread turned into a case study on identity: many commenters argued for stable national identifiers and birth-date comparisons as negation checks, while others noted that a fake entry with no identifiers to compare against defeats that fix, especially across borders. The most upvoted reflection was less about numbers and more about incentives — hyperscale platforms find it cheaper to over-block than to adjudicate, and lists built for export control were never designed for employment screening.

### Egypt's missile program and the scientist who vanished

A [Popular Mechanics feature](https://www.popularmechanics.com/military/a73358518/nazi-rocket-scientist-disappearance/) revisits Egypt in the early 1960s, when President Gamal Abdel Nasser, rebuffed by Washington and Moscow, turned to former German rocket engineers to build surface-to-surface missiles. The piece describes Factory 333 and two other sites along the Nile, the arrival of specialists including Wolfgang Pilz and Eugen Sanger, and the central role of Munich-based coordinator Heinz Krug, who sourced parts through a front company. On September 11, 1962, Krug left his office with a visitor who gave the name "Mr. Saleh" and never returned; his mud-caked car was found days later near Munich. What followed — letter bombs, shootings, and West German warnings for other scientists to stop — is presented alongside Israel's Operation Damocles, a pressure campaign to halt the program. The article lays out competing accounts of Krug's fate, from a Mossad burial in a forest to a blackmail plot involving Otto Skorzeny to the claim he lived under another name in Austria, and notes how little is settled. For the digest, the episode reads as a reminder that expertise itself traveled in Cold War networks, and that the line between coercion and collaboration could be thin.

Commenters added parallel cases — from Gerald Bull's later work to Argentinian and Libyan missile tie-ups — while several readers pushed back on monocausal narratives, arguing that institutions make choices rather than being "caused" to act.

## Science and Research

### Magnitude 7.7 earthquake off Flores, Indonesia

The [USGS executive summary](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/eventpage/us6000tkt2/executive) for the August 14, 2026 event places a magnitude 7.7 shallow reverse-fault earthquake about 68 km north-northwest of Ende, on the northern coast of East Nusa Tenggara, in a zone where the Australia plate converges on the Sunda plate. The agency notes roughly two million people felt strong to violent shaking and that damage and casualties may be significant; the area is no stranger to large events, with dozens of magnitude 6-plus quakes within 250 km since 1950, including a deadly 7.8 in 1992. Indonesian bulletins reported a small local sea-level disturbance — maximum observed wave heights under a meter, most under half a meter — and declared the tsunami threat over, consistent with the greater safety of being at sea during such swells. Aftershocks remain a concern, with a fault patch on the order of 90 by 45 km.

On Hacker News, several commenters shared real-time bulletin readings and cautioned that boarding or being near shore is the riskier part, while others pointed to long-term USGS statistics to put "a lot of big quakes this year" in context — noting that a dozen or more magnitude 7-plus events per year is within historical variability and that clustering can feel like a trend.

### An at-home test for the tick that bit you

[Smithsonian Magazine's report](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/the-first-at-home-test-for-infected-ticks-could-improve-lyme-disease-diagnosis-180989235/) profiles LymeAlert, a roughly $50 kit expected in August that tests the tick itself — not the person — for Borrelia burgdorferi, the bacterium behind Lyme disease. The kit, developed by Boston pediatric physician associate Erin Dawicki and colleagues, grinds the tick in a small crusher, treats it with a buffer, and runs an immunochromatographic strip similar in spirit to a rapid Covid or pregnancy test; an app can read the lines and connect the user to telehealth. The context, in plain terms, is scale and timing: tens of millions of tick bites a year in the U.S., hundreds of thousands treated for Lyme, and a narrow 72-hour window where a single dose of doxycycline can help after a prolonged attachment. Early blood tests miss many acute cases, and mail-in tick labs often report back too late to guide that decision; the U.S. National Tick Collection even keeps over a million specimens for research. The piece notes that warmer winters, shifting land use, and growing deer and mouse populations have expanded tick range, and that a positive tick does not mean a person is infected — only about one to five percent of bites lead to Lyme.

Commenters welcomed a fast, home option as a practical adjunct, especially in the UK where awareness is catching up to expanding risk zones; the thread's caution, echoing CDC guidance, was that a negative tick test is not a clean bill of health and that interpretation needs a clinician, with any surveillance value depending on careful use.

### A controversial Alzheimer's operation and the evidence so far

A [Nature feature](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02448-x) surveys deep cervical lymphatic-venous anastomosis — in plain terms, microsurgically connecting tiny neck lymphatic vessels to nearby veins to improve how fluid and waste drain from the brain — as proposed for Alzheimer's disease. The idea traces to the 2015 discovery of meningeal lymphatic vessels and animal work linking impaired drainage to amyloid buildup, which inspired Hangzhou microsurgeon Qingping Xie to adapt a decades-old technique used for limb swelling. A striking video of Xie's first patient in 2020 helped popularize the approach, and hundreds of hospitals in China soon offered it for upwards of $30,000 before regulators confined it to formal research settings; Xie himself has been in detention since September for undisclosed reasons. Published reports to date are mostly small, single-site, and without comparison groups, reporting modest average changes and lower amyloid and tau in spinal fluid, plus some dramatic individual recoveries — a pattern also seen in small studies elsewhere. Researchers interviewed remain divided on mechanism and durability, noting that rapid improvements are hard to square with plumbing physics and that many gains may fade within a year, with usual surgical risks of infection, bleeding, and nerve injury.

The Hacker News thread's main tension was between hope and rigor: several readers argued that even a year of improvement would matter deeply to families facing severe dementia, while others stressed the need for randomized, longer follow-up and mechanistic evidence before offering the procedure beyond trials.

## Society and Culture

This section turns from lists and labs to language, work, and how we talk to machines.

### Working with AI feels more like leading than coding

In [Working with AI feels more like leadership than coding](https://allen.bargi.org/notes/working-with-ai-feels-like-leadership/), Allen Bargi draws a simple contrast: code, for most of his career, gave certainty — same input, same result — while working with a model resembles working with people. The same request can return different outcomes, sometimes with a useful connection you hadn't considered. The essay stresses this is not about anthropomorphizing the system, which has no lived experience or accountability; it's about the work habits that help — sharing context, stating the desired outcome, setting boundaries, and iterating with examples. A good prompt helps, the piece says, but a shared working context helps more, and alignment improves over time.

Top comments argued about the word "leadership" — many said "management" fits better, noting you can delete and retry a model without worrying about morale, and that human management includes the messy, human parts no model has — while others said the experience does feel like leading when you break work into small features and steer with clear acceptance criteria. A recurring caution was that without verification, a model will happily satisfy the literal specification while missing what was actually wanted.

### A spectre is haunting Unicode

The essay [A spectre is haunting Unicode](https://www.dampfkraft.com/ghost-characters.html) tells how, in 1978, Japan's JIS X 0208 standard — the reference for later Japanese encodings — acquired several characters that no one could source or pronounce. A 1997 investigation tracked them down, often page by painstaking page in a seven-volume register of place names. Many turned out to be paste-up errors: for example, the characters for "mountain" over "woman" were printed separately, cut out, glued together, and photocopied, with the seam mistaken for a stroke to create 妛 — the correct form, 𡚴, was not encoded until much later. About a dozen core ghosts are listed — 妛挧暃椦槞蟐袮閠駲墸壥彁 — with only 彁 lacking a clear precedent, likely a misreading of 彊. Adopted into Unicode via CJK unification, these ghosts are now, potentially, on every computer.

Commenters broadened the story to unification itself: some noted that fitting CJK into early 16-bit limits forced merges that still complicate search and display, while others pointed out that historical dictionaries already contained ghost characters, and that unification's choices still echo in today's rendering defaults.

# Closing

From a name that should not have been a record to characters that should not have been letters, today kept returning to the same lesson: what gets written into a standard is hard to erase, and what gets automated at scale is hard to contest. The counterweight is careful design — in external memory for reasoning, in paste-up and proofreading for language, in ground security for aviation, and in trial design for medicine — and, in the work with AI, in stating intent clearly and checking the result. If the through-line felt sobering, the practical takeaways are modest and useful: test the tick, but confirm with a clinician; expand the notebook, but keep the verifier; fix the small hatch before rewriting the whole plane.
