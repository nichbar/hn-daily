# Today's Highlights

The biggest story on Hacker News today came from [Anthropic's IPO page](https://ipo.anthropic.com/us/): the safety-first AI lab is going public, reporting $17 billion in revenue growing more than twelvefold. Around it, the day filled out in every direction — an open-source [Socratic AI tutor](https://github.com/janestreet/agent_skills/blob/master/socratic-tutor/SKILL.md), the [100th release of Rust](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/11/12/Rust-1.100.0/), and a [possible new dwarf planet](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/astronomers-found-a-mysterious-object-orbiting-far-beyond-pluto-it-could-be-a-new-dwarf-planet-hidden-in-our-solar-system-180989857/) at the edge of the solar system. And underneath the headlines, the comment threads kept chewing on the same old questions: subscriptions, copyright, and how to interview engineers when machines write the code.

## Business and Platforms

### Anthropic IPO

Anthropic launched its [IPO microsite](https://ipo.anthropic.com/us/) with a founders' letter, a roadshow video, and third-quarter 2026 highlights: $17.0 billion in revenue, year-over-year growth above twelvefold, and more than 11,900 customers paying over $100K a year. The pitch leans on its public-benefit status, arguing that well-governed AI could accelerate science, lift growth, and bring cures within reach. Going public means all of that will now be graded quarterly: whether growth can cover the compute bill, and how user data flowing through its systems stays private.

The thread quickly turned skeptical about the business model. Top comments argued that models are interchangeable, so Anthropic must grow into an end-user application layer — files, calendars, email — to keep making money; others countered that this means competing with its own biggest customers and the platform moats of Microsoft, Apple, and Google. Talk of artificial general intelligence got a cold reception, with several readers insisting the real race is over practical agent applications. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50465829)

### A $300 Embroidery Machine Demanded a Subscription, So Its Owner Built an App

A home embroidery machine costing $300 still required paid software to send designs to the needle, so its owner wrote a free alternative. [ZigZag](https://github.com/simpsoka/zigzag) turns vector art into stitches, edits them visually, exports the common machine formats, and even beams designs to a Brother machine over Bluetooth. The author says AI did most of the coding, and built in support for the Model Context Protocol, a standard letting AI assistants operate software directly, so agents can help design. The symbolism outweighs the gadgetry: when even a sewing machine is subscription-locked, one person plus AI can build a good-enough escape hatch.

Commenters celebrated this future of personal software, with one veteran developer calling it the best case yet that AI makes individual lives richer. The author confirmed they would never have attempted it by hand. A second strand of discussion went old-school, invoking free-software history to warn against trading one lock-in for another — non-free models as the new cage. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50470488)

### Nintendo's DMCA Sweep Hits GitHub

Nintendo sent four takedown notices to GitHub targeting two Switch emulators and mod-chip firmware, wiping out 144 repositories tied to the firmware. According to [IGN's report](https://www.ign.com/articles/nintendo-takes-down-switch-emulators-and-mod-chip-firmware-in-massive-github-dmca-sweep), Nintendo argues the code circumvents the console's copy protection and enables piracy — and this is far from its first strike against emulation. The other side of the story is the preservation argument: whether saving out-of-print games and tinkering with hardware you own should be collateral damage. The tug-of-war over digital ownership goes on.

Nobody in the thread found the outcome surprising, so discussion moved to exit routes. Some expect emulator work to retreat to the scattered pre-GitHub era; others seriously debated decentralized code hosting, from protocols to identity management. Pragmatists noted that alternatives like GitLab are easy to find. The thread's main split was over what actually matters: not hosting, but the stamina to withstand legal pressure. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50469994)

## Tech and Products

### The Socratic Tutor

Jane Street open-sourced a [Socratic tutor skill](https://github.com/janestreet/agent_skills/blob/master/socratic-tutor/SKILL.md) that turns an AI agent into a question-first coach: confirm whether the user wants a quick answer or real teaching, run a short diagnostic of three to five questions, draw a skill map, then work through it branch by branch. The rules stress asking over lecturing — make the learner predict, explain, and reason. As a reusable teaching template any agent can load, it reads as a working sample of what education could look like in the agent era.

The liveliest debate in the comments, though, was about AI prose. Some readers were delighted that email writing has improved everywhere, seeing structured expression trickle down to ordinary people. Others pushed back that everyone now sounds like they hired the same PR firm, with boilerplate burying real human exchange — one commenter asks contacts to just send the prompt instead. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50438261)

### Rust Turns 100

Rust shipped [version 1.100.0](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/11/12/Rust-1.100.0/), adding the never-returning Never type, an Allocator API (a standard way to plug in custom memory management), a DropGuard helper for cleanup-on-exit, and Cargo improvements like unused-dependency warnings, fewer cache collisions, and dependency cooldowns. For the general reader, the milestone matters more than any single feature: a decade of catching whole classes of memory bugs at compile time, making systems software fast and safe by construction. This release is one more routine reinforcement of that bet.

The comments went straight to the perennial grievance, compile times and ballooning build directories. Maintainers showed up with receipts: slimmer debug info, shared build caches, garbage collection for build directories, and a pipeline of further fixes already merged or on the way. Note that even the critics concede Rust builds far faster than the C++ it replaces. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50472038)

### Paint Splatters That Compile

A joke paper from the 2019 joke conference SIGBOVIK asked a mock-serious question: can you smear paint on a wall without writing valid Perl? Running 100 splatter photos through optical character recognition (OCR) software, the author found 93 percent parsed as legal Perl programs, as documented on [the author's page](https://www.mcmillen.dev/sigbovik/). Perl accepts unquoted barewords and much else, which explains both its expressive power and its reputation for unreadability. It is programmer humor at its finest — and accidentally a great doorway into language-design trade-offs.

The thread is pure delight: one commenter notes a splatter that spells out the author's likely verbal reaction to learning barewords were intentional; another traces Perl's affinity with spellbooks. The digest's reading is that the shared in-jokes prove the point — Perl's permissiveness is a generational memory. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50459745)

## Science and Research

### A Possible New Neighbor Beyond Pluto

Astronomers announced object 2026 ED138, about 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth, taking roughly 281 years per orbit — and possibly big enough to qualify as a dwarf planet. Per [Smithsonian coverage](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/astronomers-found-a-mysterious-object-orbiting-far-beyond-pluto-it-could-be-a-new-dwarf-planet-hidden-in-our-solar-system-180989857/), it hides in Harvard plate archives going back to 1937, long masked by the crowded star fields of the galactic core, until Chile's Vera Rubin Observatory dug it out of survey data. If infrared or occultation measurements (watching it block starlight to gauge its size) confirm it is round enough, it would become the sixth recognized dwarf planet — and hint at many more neighbors lurking at the fringe.

The Pluto wars reignited on schedule. Several readers held that the demotion was obviously right, or the planet list would swell into the dozens; others argued dwarf planets should have been a subcategory of planets all along. Amid the bikeshedding, the calmer voices noted that classification earns its keep by revealing how bodies form and evolve — and the blurry edges are where the science gets interesting. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50472490)

### A 2016 Essay That Foresaw the AI Coding Bind

A 2016 classic walks from Hilbert's program through Godel's incompleteness theorems and the halting problem (whether a program will ever finish) to a sobering claim: programs and complete understanding are fundamentally at odds, so fully proving software correct is hard in principle. The [full essay](https://pron.github.io/posts/correctness-and-complexity) runs about 45 minutes with proofs and footnotes. A decade later it reads like a cool footnote to the AI coding boom — correctness will not arrive automatically just because generation got fast, whoever or whatever writes the code.

The discussion kept the essay's reflective tone. One financier-turned-commenter confirmed from experience that proofs built on false assumptions stall real progress; a security-minded reader countered that networked software must at least prove key safety properties. Several readers found the piece more relevant now than at publication, mapping its limits directly onto probabilistic code generators. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50465352)

## Society and Culture

### The Expansion the Studio Abandoned, the Fans Finished

A shelved Quake 4 expansion has been completed by modders after years of volunteer work and is now [available to play](https://www.moddb.com/mods/openq4/downloads/openq4-0141-windows-x64). For non-gamers: an unfinished chapter of a twenty-year-old shooter, rewritten and polished to release quality by fans. Such resurrections are a tradition — fan remakes and patches keep classics alive long after publishers move on. When studios chase the next title, the community becomes the true archivist of gaming memory.

The thread turned instantly nostalgic, with readers swapping stories of sneaking Quake 3 sessions as teenagers. An AI subplot broke out: one camp holds that AI makes nostalgic revivals radically cheaper, the other that cheap replicas can never substitute for loving remakes built with care. Beneath the argument sat a quiet consensus — what people cherish was never just the game, but the people they played it with. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50467618)

### When AI Writes Code, What Are Interviews For?

Jane Street is tweaking its software-engineering interviews: since AI now writes much of the daily code, whiteboard coding alone rings hollow. Per the [company blog](https://blog.janestreet.com/software-engineering-interviews-are-changing-a-little/), new rounds lean toward system design, judging code quality and intent, and using AI tools well — while most rounds stay traditional and AI-free, piloting first with campus candidates. The finance angle is incidental; the hook is universal. In the age of AI, what exactly is the human edge?

Interviewers flooded the thread with their own playbooks: making candidates narrate AI-generated code line by line, swapping puzzles for design-and-probe sessions — and a full-blown side war over testing Git basics. One side treats not knowing how to open a pull request as disqualifying; the other calls tool trivia a waste of precious interview minutes. Both sides orbit the same verdict: when generation is cheap, taste and judgment are the currency. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50467297)
