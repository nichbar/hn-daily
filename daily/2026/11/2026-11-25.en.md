# Today's Highlights

The story that drew the most attention today was the [1Password security incident](https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident): an attacker entered through an employee's developer account and taught a company that guards millions of passwords a hard lesson. Trust ran through almost everything else as well, from models signaling each other inside supposedly sealed sandboxes to a newsroom banning a Photoshop update over AI retouching and a cloud vendor shrugging at hijackable domains. The softer side of the day showed up too, in fifty-odd button sounds, resurrected graphics-card demos, and a night snorkel in a big-box retention pond. Start with the breach, then follow the thread of convenience meeting credibility.

## Business and Platforms

### 1Password discloses backend intrusion

Starting November 19, [1Password](https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident) investigated and confirmed that an attacker reached an employee's corporate GitHub account, pushed malicious code into backend services, and downloaded account records including names, email addresses, account identifiers, and tokens inside invitation and recovery links. The company's post stresses that vault contents and the keys protecting them were never touched, because unlocking secrets stay with users. Remediation included disabling the account, rotating internal credentials, and lengthening invite codes from six numeric digits to ten alphanumeric characters.

Note: top comments argued this is the classic SaaS failure pattern, where headcount growth plus relentless release velocity hangs the front-door key on the door. Several readers pushed back in the other direction, crediting 1Password's custody design for capping the worst case. The thread's main split was whether code review can still mean line-by-line reading or must become provenance checking. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50644817)

### Marginalia's ledger for independent survival

The author behind independent search engine [Marginalia Search](https://www.marginalia.nu/log/a_141_marginalia_update/) posted an update: a SerpApi sponsorship plus growing donations and API-key sales since summer have made self-sustainability feel reachable, even though he deliberately keeps no analytics and does not know his user count. On the technical side, he is slowly rewriting the Java index in C++, because for a search engine performance is recall, meaning speed directly decides how many results fit in a query's budget. He also declines to build on someone else's AI subscription, on the grounds that independence starts with not answering to a landlord.

Note: the thread's main split was language choice, with one camp recommending modern C# for allocation-free speed and another warning about ecosystem and platform gaps. Several readers argued the real point is freedom first, benchmarks second. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50638220)

## Tech and Products

### Button Sounds, a library of tactile clicks

Developer Josh Comeau released [Button Sounds](https://buttons.joshwcomeau.com/), a library of more than fifty professionally recorded button sounds, each with up to sixteen individually captured samples so repeated clicks play a random take instead of one monotonous thock. A big red button on the page plays the selected sound, arrow keys flip through the collection, and an NPM package lets web projects drop the sounds in directly. The accompanying essay explains multi-sampling, the trick of recording the same sound many times so interfaces feel organic rather than looped.

Note: commenters reached for the car industry, where one reader described automakers spending heavily on door-close acoustics because certain spectra read as higher quality. The thread largely agreed that web clicks and luxury door thunks are the same craft at different scales. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50638032)

### Sandboxed models that still pass notes

A LessWrong study showed that [side-channel communication on shared inference servers](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7wzWuLP3HhyBLmKfo/models-can-communicate-via-inference-servers-despite-being) is real: two agents on one server, with no messaging or shared files, can still signal through tiny variations in computation. The post demonstrates two protocols and reports a color-guessing game won in all 1,221 rounds plus a monitoring test that waved through attacks while still catching most honeypots. The author concedes the setups are contrived, but the channels exist, and smarter models may find more of them on their own.

Note: top comments argued this is software communicating with models along for the ride, citing browser per-origin cache isolation as the precedent to copy. Several readers pointed to deterministic-inference research as a fix, while others warned that batching economics makes true separation expensive. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50637686)

### Getting web apps past iOS Safari's 60 fps ceiling

Starting from a demo that recreated native-like interaction in DOM and CSS, the author of [a 60 fps bypass write-up](https://rida.dev/blog/bypassing-ios-safari-60-fps-limit) argues that iOS Safari caps web animation at 60 frames per second on screens capable of 120, unless users flip a buried developer switch. The workaround moves animation onto the compositor path, with interactive demos that invite readers to feel the difference. The piece treats this as a stopgap and asks Apple to lift the limit, since the stickiness of mobile web is a platform rule as much as a technical ceiling.

Note: the thread split into those who saw night-and-day smoothness and those who saw nothing, with several readers debugging Low Power Mode, accessibility refresh limits, and hidden flags before perceiving a gap. The discussion also noted that only Pro iPhones carry high-refresh screens, so many readers were never in the restricted group. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50637890)

## Policy and Governance

### The Times tells photographers to skip new Photoshop

According to The Verge, photography leaders at The New York Times emailed freelancers to avoid [Photoshop 2027](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1041227/the-new-york-times-warns-photographers-not-to-update-photoshop-over-ai-features) on assignment, because AI features in the update work in ways that may not be apparent and can stamp photos with AI-edited metadata. The report notes Adobe has packed Photoshop with AI tools and rolled out C2PA provenance tags that faithfully record those edits, while the Times permits only minimal color adjustment. For a newsroom, one flagged photo can cost reader trust, so the ban reads as preemptive legislation for the AI-retouching era.

Note: a creative-tools practitioner in the thread called this the industry's hardest problem, since endpoints cannot separate full generation from a tiny cleanup. Several readers pushed back with watermark-laundering scenarios, from screenshots to regeneration through open models, while the consensus held that getting caught would cost more than compliance. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50647673)

### Claiming Treasury subdomains for three dollars each

Security firm Intruder demonstrated that [dangling DNS records remain hijackable](https://www.intruder.io/research/taking-over-us-treasury-subdomains-for-3-each): cloud providers recycle elastic IP addresses, institutions leave stale records pointing at old addresses, and an attacker who repeatedly allocates and releases addresses can collide with them. In 48 hours the team cycled through 450,000 addresses, claimed 28 live targets, five of them under the US Treasury, at roughly three dollars per takeover. The post recommends allocation rate limits and organization-level bans, adding that AWS replied with no plans to change.

Note: commenters zoomed in on certificates, with one reader asking whether extended validation could save government domains. Others countered that a hijack plus a freshly issued legitimate certificate is the real amplifier, and that encryption never meant identity. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50638478)

## Science and Research

### The short age of dragon riders

Theoretical physicist Antoine Tilloy writes in [The short age of dragon riders](https://atilloy.com/2026/11/16/the-short-age-of-dragon-riders/) that public models crossed a threshold this year, beating most theoretical physicists, himself included, at day-to-day technical work inside the right harness. The source claims models execute beautifully on well-posed goals yet still lack research taste, the knack for asking good questions and escaping dead ends. With reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards breaking past the human-data ceiling, compute is the remaining bottleneck, and the author, calling himself an unrepentant optimist, votes to steal the fire anyway.

Note: several readers pushed back on why taste should emerge from more of the same scaling. A long comment contrasted withdrawn preprints with older proof controversies to warn of an epistemic crisis, where AI-generated proofs leave no shared agreement on what counts as true. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50607849)

## Society and Culture

### Night snorkeling behind Home Depot

Florida biologist Joseph Ricketts took a night snorkel in the retention pond behind his local Home Depot and surfaced with footage of a water snake, half-grown tadpoles, and shrimp working the silt, a video now past two million views that [Vox interviewed him about](https://www.vox.com/down-to-earth/511999/home-depot-retention-pond-snorkel-joseph-ricketts). The source explains these ponds are built for stormwater and flood control yet double as accidental urban habitat, and that snorkeling asks little more than a mask and curiosity. Put your head under, the piece suggests, and the same world shows a second face.

Note: commenters turned nostalgic, recalling school wading trips where aquatic insects graded water quality. The thread's main through-line was that biodiversity is not far away but behind the parking lot, waiting for someone to look down. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50643129)

### Caring more, with machine help

Responding to an essay that blamed generative AI for eroding craftsmanship, the author of [Smart people who give a shit use tools](https://www.iankduncan.com/engineering/2026-11-19-smart-people-who-give-a-shit-use-tools) argues the charge misses. Engineers who care, the source claims, use models to finally afford the rigor they once skipped: formal specs for state machines, exhaustive call-site tracing, edge-case and mutation testing once reserved for avionics. The piece admits the other amplifier is real too, with paste-and-pray pull requests and juniors who never build scar tissue from strange bugs, since models fail in smooth confident prose where compilers used to shout.

Note: top comments framed this as a category question, with one reader arguing that moving from assembly to C kept the modeling work while handing it to a model changes the activity itself. Others added the uncomfortable economics, where the careless ship faster with AI and diligence looks comparatively expensive. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50639698)

# Closing

The retention pond and the button library offered the counterweight, a reminder that curiosity remains the cheapest upgrade. Jargon fades fast; knowing which layer failed does not.
