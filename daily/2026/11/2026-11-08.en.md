# Today's Highlights

The top story on Hacker News yesterday was [KetchUp](https://ketchup3d.github.io/app/), an open-source 3D modeling tool written in Rust and WebGPU that focuses on woodworking and furniture design. It is free, requires no account, and runs right in the browser. The discussion around it quickly grew into a bigger argument about whether the AI era makes it practical to build your own tools. The day's other top threads followed a similar theme of reflecting on tech-shaped work: the open-source Linux desktop [Dux](https://duxlabs.dev/) shipped a new version that rekindled a debate about Linux desktop philosophy, and a blog post about staying focused while juggling AI coding agents resonated with readers across backgrounds.

## Tech and Products

### [KetchUp](https://ketchup3d.github.io/app/): open-source 3D furniture design in your browser

KetchUp is built with Rust and WebGPU, the browser graphics standard that makes complex 3D rendering run smoothly without plugins. It works in a web browser or as a native Windows, macOS, or Linux app, with no account and no cost. The target audience is specific: woodworkers, furniture makers, and DIY builders who want to see a shelf, table, or cabinet take shape before making the first cut. The workflow feels familiar to anyone who has used a 3D modeler — draw a rectangle, pull it into a board, type exact dimensions, then orbit around the assembly to check proportions. It can import SketchUp geometry and export the widely supported OBJ format, and all files stay on your device by default. The project is MIT-licensed and has since been renamed BenchUp.

The most interesting part of the HN thread was not the product but the debate it triggered about whether it makes sense to build your own tools with AI. Several commenters argued that asking Claude or Codex to generate a small custom 3D editor is now faster than learning Blender or SketchUp; one person described having an AI assemble a functional subset of Blender in a few hours. Others pushed back, warning that maintaining your own tool is far costlier than it looks — edge cases, quirks, and missing features quietly eat your time — and that mature 3D software remains hard to replace. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50418176).

### [Dux](https://duxlabs.dev/) 2.1 "Stormborn": a growing open-source desktop, and a debate about scope

Dux is a free, open-source desktop environment for Linux, built on the Wayland display protocol. Version 2.1 adds a system monitor, video wallpapers, a revamped media player, and an automated theming system that derives matching 16-color palettes for GTK and Qt apps, terminals, and editors. The wider Dux family includes a file manager, calendar, search, and login screen, and the project counts more than 600 contributors.

In the HN discussion, several users praised the shell as "extremely solid," while others worried it is becoming too all-encompassing. The thread's main split was between fans of comprehensive desktops and users who prefer minimal, focused compositors — some noted they are switching to lighter options like Noctalia or River. A few readers also pointed out that open source should be a given for Linux desktops, recalling the KDE era debates over Qt's licensing. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50416053).

## Society and Culture

### [How I Fight AI Brain Fry](https://sergeykuhne.com/blog/fighting-ai-brain-fry): staying focused amid a swarm of AI agents

This blog post names a problem many workers now know firsthand: when you run several AI coding agents at once, you end up hopping between dozens of chat threads, and your attention collapses. The author calls it "AI brain fry." His proposed fix is a task-queue workflow: every task is a Markdown card stored in a git repository, an agent processes the queue and runs tests, and anything needing a human decision lands in a single "owner inbox." The author deliberately works on one item at a time and closes its context when done, so nothing lingers in working memory.

The post struck a chord. Top comments agreed with the "do less, with more focus" approach — several engineers said they cap their concurrent sessions at two or three and use waiting time to sketch or take notes, while one described a rhythm of planning, fanning work out to agents, then reviewing and testing in concentrated bursts. But the thread also turned critical: some commenters said they lose interest the moment a text smells AI-generated, and others defended writing as a way to order your own thinking, warning against becoming a person who only delegates. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50416363).
