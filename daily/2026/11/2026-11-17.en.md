# Today's Highlights

The biggest story of the day was [OpenAI's GPT-6.2 launch](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/): a flagship model claiming record professional-task results at a fraction of the cost, paired with a cheaper everyday companion, and suddenly everyone was discounting intelligence. The rest rewarded the curious: chemistry as geometry, moonlight measuring the speed of light, and an open-source community arguing about AI-generated pull requests.

## Tech and Products

### GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol are here

[OpenAI's announcement](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-2-astra-sol/) brings two models at once: the flagship Astra for demanding professional work, said to produce slides, spreadsheets, and documents that follow field conventions, write cleaner code, and build better frontends, games, and 3D designs, with new records on the multi-step Agents' Last Exam and two science benchmarks; and the lighter Sol for everyday office tasks. The shared pitch is fewer tokens per job, tokens being the text chunks models bill by, with Astra's API prices cut by a fifth and cached input discounted by up to three-fifths. Top comments called the price-to-performance startling, timed conspicuously ahead of Anthropic's IPO, while several readers pushed back that benchmarks and lived experience keep diverging, with one linking a rough third-party field test and asking which leaderboard actually predicts daily coding life.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50535230).

### One poisoned vendor turned 178 big sites into malware carriers

Security firm [Report URI's report](https://blog.report-uri.com/worlds-largest-brands-infected-with-malware-via-optimizely/) reads like a thriller: starting November 16, visitors to 178 organizations' sites received malicious scripts through the Optimizely web experimentation platform, with Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab, IKEA, HSBC, and others among the recognizable names. The payload swapped crypto wallet addresses, skimmed payment cards, stole cookies and screenshots, and served iOS exploit kits to Apple devices, with confirmed crypto theft already on record. The source says a trusted third-party script was compromised, the kind of anomaly a properly enforced content security policy, a browser rule limiting which scripts a page may run, should have caught. The thread's main split was over blame: many readers indicted the tag-manager culture that lets marketing teams add code without engineers, some argued serving malware should carry criminal liability, and others asked what that would mean for poisoned open-source projects.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524767).

### Stop shipping apps, start fixing open source

Freelance developer [Jordan Krueger's plea](https://jordankrueger.com/blog/better-open-source-not-more-apps/) names the awkwardness of the AI era: every day someone demos yet another weekend-built notepad, which delights him, but he wishes some of that energy flowed into the open-source software everyone depends on. Running his own chat server and budget-hosted apps, he writes, taught him the pain is rarely features but installation and upkeep: setup guides that assume five other tools, tiny apps eating gigabytes of memory, upgrades that break at midnight. His ask is concrete: pick one open-source tool you use, spend a weekend fixing its most annoying setup problem with the maintainers. Maintainers in the thread largely agreed the flood of speculative, AI-assisted pull requests is drowning triage, with one warning of closing repos to contributions; other readers countered that most patches still come from real pain, and the real question is whether collaboration norms need rewriting for AI.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50532086).

## Policy and Governance

### Windows 11 found logging which AI tools you use

Independent researcher [Xusheng's reverse-engineering writeup](https://xusheng.dev/posts/windows-coding-agent-telemetry/) uncovered a privacy thread: a Windows 11 system component keeps a roster naming AI coding tools such as Codex, Claude, Copilot, and Cursor alongside developer tools like Python, dotnet, and gcc. The system records whether they have been used, writes the findings to a local diagnostics report, and uploads it with the opt-out diagnostic data stream that ships enabled by default. The author says an AI assistant helped trace the lead from string matches to the live reporting component, with an intact evidence chain. The discussion quickly became a distro-hopping clinic: several readers argued most single-player games now run faster on Linux, with kernel-level anti-cheat the only real barrier, while others advised switching off the optional diagnostics immediately.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524736).

### The NSA's classified internal network goes down

[CNN's exclusive](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/highly-classified-national-security-communications-network-goes-down) says the National Security Agency's classified internal communications net, NSANet, went down on Tuesday evening, with the campus closed to all but essential staff and an earlier all-clear message reversed. Six people familiar with the matter confirmed the outage to CNN; the network carries internal classified traffic. The agency's statement called it temporary degradation during scheduled maintenance, insisted mission capabilities stayed intact, and said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. Commenters strung the outage together with recent airline groundings, a Microsoft outage, and bank login failures into a pattern-seeking list, while the calmer voices explained that such networks' existence and status were never secret, only the data on them, and a campus shutdown is simply too loud to hide.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50532849).

### A French court ruling drives out free DNS

[Cloudflare's defense of a peer](https://blog.cloudflare.com/quad9-withdrawal-from-france/) takes sides: Swiss nonprofit Quad9 is withdrawing its free public DNS resolver from France after a court ordered it to block piracy-linked domains under threat of daily fines. DNS translates domain names into addresses, and the post argues that enforcing blocks at this layer harms privacy and security, fragments the open internet, and never reaches the piracy source. The warning is that if rightsholders and policymakers keep demanding blocking at every layer, the French case becomes Europe's bad template. Quad9's CTO showed up in the thread, saying nobody outside the rightsholder industry at his standards meeting backed the outcome and urging Europeans to write long-form objections to their policymakers; most readers agreed blocking belongs at the source, not in the phone book.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50538994).

## Science and Research

### Chemistry is actually geometry

A Substack science writer [explains chemistry through geometry](https://chillphysicsenjoyer.substack.com/p/chemistry-is-actually-geometry): starting from Feinberg's deficiency theorems, a result from theoretical biology, drawing a reaction network as shapes can predict whether reactions coexist stably without solving thousands of differential equations. Since life itself is robust chemistry, this geometric intuition could shortcut the design of life-like synthetic systems. The piece carries a do-real-science-at-home spirit, turning a theorem from theoretical biology into a story for non-specialists. The thread split philosophically: one camp warned against mistaking the map for the territory, with string theory cited as math that nature never chose, while the other camp argued industrial schooling slices knowledge too thin and exactly this kind of cross-field analogy is what is missing.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522643).

### Three hundred fifty years ago, a late moon measured light's speed

Norway's scienceNorway revisits [Rømer's elegant wager](https://www.sciencenorway.no/culture-history-history-of-science/350-years-ago-this-man-discovered-that-light-has-a-finite-speed/2737969): in September 1676, Danish astronomer Ole Rømer predicted to the Paris Academy that on November 9, Jupiter's moon Io would emerge from shadow ten minutes late, and he was right. The only explanation was that light needs different times to cross different distances, so its speed is finite, overturning the belief that light arrives instantly and turning a moon into humanity's first light-speed clock. Commenters detoured from Rømer to the one-way speed of light chestnut: isotropy cannot be measured directly, only round trips, with one reader likening it to network latency, where without an outside clock like GPS you cannot tell which leg is slow, to the delight of physicists and engineers alike.
