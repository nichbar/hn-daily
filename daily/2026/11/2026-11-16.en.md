## Tech and Products

### Qwen's new flagship wants to be your teammate, not your chatbot

The Qwen team has released [Qwen4-Max](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen4-max), pitched as an "AI teammate": a 2.4-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts architecture (a design that splits the model into specialist sub-networks and activates only some of them per task, saving compute) with a million-token context window. The official numbers claim across-the-board gains over its predecessor on coding, long-horizon tasks, and a real-office benchmark, with roughly 30% lower token usage and per-task cost down from $4.43 to $1.67. The emphasis on usable deliverables in 3D modeling, game development, finance, and legal work signals where the race is heading: from chatting well to finishing work, with falling prices pushing that shift into everyday use.

Note: those figures are the vendor's own, so treat them as a claim to be verified rather than settled fact. On HN, developer Simon Willison promptly posted pelican SVGs generated at different "reasoning effort" settings, running the new model head-to-head against its predecessor for a visual comparison. Several readers welcomed the efficiency gains as concrete progress, while the thread's main split was between enthusiasts and wait-for-independent-benchmarks skeptics. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513215).

### The ad blocker you trust just fixed a one-click takeover bug

A security researcher has disclosed a universal cross-site scripting flaw in [the full Firefox build of uBlock Origin](https://iter.ca/post/ubo-uxss/): a malicious site could silently embed the extension's own element picker, trick the user into clicking an invisible button, and plant a trusted filter rule that injects code into any website. One click, in other words, could have handed over the whole browser. There is an ironic footnote: AI did most of the heavy lifting in finding the bug and drafting the proof of concept, with the human researcher verifying and writing it up. The issue is fixed now, so updating the extension is all most people need to do.

The thread quickly moved from the bug itself to a structural complaint: extension interfaces have to live inside the page's DOM, which lets pages snoop on or manipulate them, and password managers share the exposure. Longtime Firefox users recalled the old XUL extension architecture with its cleaner separation, though several readers pushed back that today's web is far too complex to go back. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50513160).

### Outage after outage: bad luck, or software rotting from the inside

Blogger Breck-McKye looked at the pileup of 2026 incidents, the Microsoft global outage, the FAA ground stop, a critical PHP remote-execution bug, a certificate-authority weak-key fiasco, and asked whether [software is breaking down at a systemic level](https://www.breck-mckye.com/blog/2026/11/software-is-breaking-down-at-a-systemic-level/). The causes differ, the piece argues, but the shape rhymes. Its central concept is "decoherence": AI makes every snippet look reasonable while prompts wrap prompts and summaries summarize summaries, with nobody left responsible for the whole. Complexity grows faster than humans can tame it, and organizations lose the ability to think as one. The starkest warning is societal: we have entrusted banking, flights, and power grids to software without preparing for a world where such failures are routine.

Top comments largely endorsed the diagnosis but split on the prescription. One camp blamed people and process, arguing teams that skip documentation get exactly the AI output they deserve, with strict process as the cure. The gloomier camp held that AI-driven decoherence reinforces itself and no checklist will fix it. A few engineers confessed they can no longer code without AI assistance, which the thread read as either adaptation or surrender depending on who was talking. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520866).

## Business and Platforms

### Amazon's "deals" investigation: a third offered no savings at all

A new report from the Groundwork Collaborative examined hundreds of products Amazon promoted as top [Black Friday and Cyber Monday "deals"](https://groundworkcollaborative.org/news/amazons-decoy-deals-groundwork-investigation-reveals-nearly-1-in-3-black-friday-and-cyber-monday-deals-advertised-by-amazon-offered-no-savings-to-shoppers-nearly-1-in-10-w/) and found nearly one in three sold for the same price or less in the month before the sale, with almost one in ten priced higher during the event itself. The report's reading is that crossed-out list prices and "lowest ever" badges often exploit anchoring: shoppers feel they grabbed a bargain while paying the everyday price. As sale seasons stretch longer each year, the practical takeaway is unglamorous but real: check the price history before checking out.

The HN thread turned into a savings-tips swap. Several readers said killing Prime cut their impulse spending dramatically and that waiting to hit free-shipping thresholds beats sale-chasing. Others pushed back that they never impulse-buy and stay for unmatched delivery speed. Beneath the disagreement sat a shared verdict: the rules of the sale game keep getting more complicated, and uninformed shoppers pay the tuition. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50517488).

### One software defect, several banks on the ropes

The Associated Press reports that [a software defect in an on-premises hardware system at JPMorgan Chase](https://apnews.com/article/bank-outage-chase-wells-fargo-truist-50fee2693c22f597fa6ca51a97ef2cda) cascaded into widespread delays at Chase, Wells Fargo, Truist, and the Zelle transfer network, with customers locked out of online accounts and ATMs misbehaving. Chase stressed it was not a cyberattack and aimed to restore service the same day. The timing amplified the story: it landed days after the Microsoft 365 outage, a United Airlines systems failure, and an FAA ground stop. A bank outage hits differently from a flaky app, and the "are critical systems getting more fragile" debate promptly went mainstream.

Commenters divided into familiar trenches. One side posted search-trend charts of declining software quality and blamed AI-generated code plus outsourcing. The other side countered with availability bias: failures make the news, and overall uptime is far better than a decade ago. The most interesting voices landed in between, arguing the rot sits at the seams: every team ships faster, but nobody coordinates the boundaries where systems meet. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50521045).

### After Huang: can Nvidia reinvent itself a fourth time

Startup mentor Steve Blank has written a widely shared essay on [Nvidia after Jensen Huang's resignation](https://steveblank.com/2026/11/15/whats-next-for-nvidia/): across thirty-three years the founder reinvented the company three times, turning graphics chips into a general computing platform with CUDA, betting on deep learning, and acquiring Mellanox to sell whole AI data centers. Each was a business-model transformation, not just a product launch. Blank's warning to the board, drawn from the Microsoft and Apple successions, is that execution CEOs preserve while visionary CEOs reshape, and Nvidia's real risk is losing the ability to create the next market rather than missing a quarter. Whoever takes over, and whether they will bet big on the next discontinuity, shapes the next decade of the whole AI wave.

The thread ran with the "disrupt yourself or be disrupted" theme. The top comment invoked Jobs killing the iPod with the iPhone, arguing Nvidia should buy the startups that burn models into specialized silicon and cannibalize its own GPU sales before rivals do. Skeptics answered with post-Jobs Apple: you can stop reinventing and still become the most profitable company on earth. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50512235).

## Science and Research

### "Checked in Lean" — what does that actually prove

Mathematician Kevin Buzzard has written a plain-English guide to [interpreting claims that a theorem is correct because it was checked in Lean](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/) (Lean being a proof assistant that turns mathematical arguments into machine-checkable code). The context is OpenAI's release of 722 mathematics papers, nearly half with accompanying Lean code. His four questions: does the formalized statement actually match the paper's theorem, since AI can mistranscribe conditions or prove only a lemma; does the code compile on your machine, at hundreds of thousands of lines; could the proof smuggle in redefinitions or extra axioms, which needs dedicated checking tools; and could it exploit a bug in the prover's core, which needs cross-checking with independent kernels. His verdict: Lean verification is a strong signal, but "it compiles" is not victory.

The thread's favorite translation came from programmers: a checked proof is like code that compiles, meaning no syntax errors, with no guarantee the program does what you wanted. The key is always whether the statement was formalized faithfully. Several readers added that this is the new AI-era normal: machines now produce proofs faster than human reviewers can absorb them, making reviewer attention the scarcest resource. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50515058).

### Sheep, bees, and solar panels can all win at once

Two Cornell studies have put numbers on [grazing sheep beneath solar arrays](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/10/sheep-grazing-solar-fields-offers-farmers-economic-ecological-gains): at two sheep per hectare, beneficial insects including bees and lady beetles rose 38% versus mowed control plots, with honeybees alone up sharply. Four sheep per hectare kept the ecological gains while managing vegetation better; at eight, honeybee numbers fell off. A companion study adds a decision tool to help farmers size their flocks profitably. The researchers' framing reframes the question from whether to build solar on grassland to how: find the stocking-density sweet spot and power, livestock, and pollinators all benefit.

The thread's inevitable question was why grassland at all when deserts are empty. The answer, several readers explained, is transmission: plants near population centers avoid hugely expensive long-distance lines, and grassland tends to sit near people. Others pointed to China's ultra-high-voltage lines carrying desert power east as proof it is less about physics than infrastructure and permitting trade-offs. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50520182).

### Full-body tracking from a single smartwatch, no camera needed

Cornell's SciFi Lab has unveiled [EchoMotion](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2026/11/researchers-track-whole-body-movement-just-smartwatch), which uses an ordinary smartwatch's speaker and microphone to bathe the wearer in inaudible ultrasound and infer the positions of twelve joints, head to ankles, from the echoes. Error runs around four inches, cut by a third after a twelve-minute calibration, and the same model worked across different watch brands without retraining. No camera means privacy in the gym and tiny power draw: half an hour cost just a few percent of battery. The team has tested it in small rooms, hallways, and noisy streets, and wants AI trainers to count reps and correct form next, with physical rehabilitation in view too.

Fitness-minded commenters said they have wanted exactly this, noting some trackers already guess at lifts and asking how the acoustic approach compares. The privacy camp cheered the camera-free design: in a half-public gym, sound waves feel far less creepy than lenses. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50486745).

## Society and Culture

### CMU puts whole semesters of computer science on YouTube, free

[Carnegie Mellon has launched "CS Learn"](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/learn/), releasing full semesters of current computer science lectures on YouTube, one course premiering each day this week with more in January and free courseware to follow. The project lead, instructor Phillip Compeau, says students built tooling that automates much of production, letting a small team ship courses fast with human review on every lecture. Early offerings span AI, robotics, systems, and biology across seven departments. A quarter-century after MIT's open courseware bet, the elite classroom keeps moving toward public infrastructure.

Note: the launch details come partly from Compeau himself, who showed up in the HN thread to explain the backstory and solicit topic requests. Readers asked for PeerTube mirrors alongside YouTube and got a receptive answer. The liveliest sub-debate asked whether elite lectures still matter in the LLM era; defenders argued solid fundamentals never expire and better source material makes AI answers better too. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50522980).

### MIT's "make almost anything": in the AI era, is breadth the new depth

MIT's famous [“How to Make (Almost) Anything” course](https://pub.cba.mit.edu/863.26/site/) has posted its new syllabus, spanning computer-aided design, computer-controlled cutting, embedded programming, and electronics, one theme per week, hands-on throughout. The course has always been proudly broad rather than deep: a panoramic map of fabrication methods rather than mastery of one. Critics call that sightseeing; defenders say the timing has never been better. When AI agents can implement the details, they argue, knowing what tools exist and what problems they solve is itself the scarce skill, and creativity comes from a sense of the possible, not fluency in minutiae.

Top comments made the case for panoramic education: encountering a technique once means knowing where to look when problem X arrives, with one reader's plumbing apprenticeship as the analogy. Others cited pop-science classics that work the same way. Skeptics found the syllabus a buzzword list, to which defenders replied this is only the outline: the real learning happens in lab hours with machines. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50514470).

### A 2007 laptop incantation that still resurrects dead machines

In 2007, Mike Masnick faced every speaker's nightmare: his brand-new ThinkPad died an hour before his talk, no lights, no response, battery pulls useless. IBM support gave him a ritual that sounded like a prank: unplug everything, [press the power button ten times, hold it for thirty seconds to discharge static, then reassemble](https://masnick.com/posts/the-secret-thinkpad-powerbutton-code-to-bring-dead-laptops-back-to-life/), and the machine came back to life. Nearly two decades later the post resurfaced because the legend says it has saved countless machines since, and the comments became instant testimony: a dropped gaming laptop revived on the spot, plus call-center psychology explaining why support says "ten times" (because customers asked to hold for three seconds hold for one).

Former phone-support staff in the thread confirmed the trick from the other side of the headset, and several readers noted the principle generalizes: drain residual charge before declaring any laptop dead. It is the rare internet fossil that still earns its keep. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50516473).
