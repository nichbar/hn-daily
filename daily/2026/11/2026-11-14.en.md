# Today's Highlights

The most spectacular moment of the day came from Texas: a spacecraft that had spent more than 20 hours circling Earth dropped back through the clouds and was plucked out of the air by a pair of giant mechanical arms. [Starship's 15th flight](https://www.spacex.com/launches/starship-flight-15) marks the first time the ship itself has been caught, and fully reusable giant rockets suddenly look like routine operations rather than stunts.

The other half of the day's conversation faced the opposite direction: what happens when technology runs faster than its safety nets. AI-generated websites leaked real passports and chat logs, smart glasses record everyone nearby with no opt-out for bystanders, and a night of grounded planes left even train tickets unsellable. Today's threads kept circling the same question from different angles: grand engineering deserves applause, but ordinary people's situations deserve a close look too.

## Science and Research

### [Starship's 15th flight](https://www.spacex.com/launches/starship-flight-15): a ship caught midair for the first time

On November 13, Starship lifted off from Starbase, Texas, with 33 engines pushing the booster skyward before the upper-stage ship headed for orbit and released 26 Starlink V3 satellites. The booster ended its return burn early over a fuel issue and diverted itself to a sea landing; the real history came about 22.5 hours later, when the ship returned from its long orbital coast, fired its engines to slow down, and was caught by the launch tower's arms, nicknamed Mechazilla. SpaceX's verdict: this changes everything. What observers are watching next is less romantic but just as decisive: what a heat-shield refurbishment costs, whether the engines survive repeated thermal cycling, and when orbital refueling (topping up a ship's tanks in space) arrives.

The thread turned into a space-history debate. Some readers called Starship the Shuttle done right, arguing a few hundred flights would settle the reliability question on their own; others countered with Apollo reaching the Moon in a handful of launches, only to be reminded that Apollo 1 killed three astronauts and iteration always has a price. The digest's reading: both sides are really arguing about the same gap, the one between spectacle and routine. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50500963).

### [Elephants are growing lonely](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/elephants-found-to-be-increasingly-isolated-leading-to-fractured-social-networks): a cover-story warning from Science

A UC San Diego team spent 12 years tracking more than 400 female Asian elephants in a Sri Lankan national park and found the population had crossed a social tipping point. What were once large, tightly linked networks have fractured into small, isolated clusters, and the remaining elephants have changed not just how many friends they have but how they make friends at all. Poaching, conflict with people, fragmented habitat, and shrinking food supplies are all driving elephants away, with almost no newcomers arriving. The study made the cover of the November 12 issue of Science, and its authors warn the fracturing is hard to reverse, while offering one hope: monitoring social patterns could sound the alarm before more species collapse.

Several readers drew parallels to orcas and to humans, asking whether loneliness is the same disease across intelligent species. A reader from Sri Lanka added that the human-elephant conflict remains unsolved, with bloodshed on both sides. Others noticed the paper's careful wording around climate change and suspected caution, though the math of shrinking habitat speaks for itself. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50492860).

## Tech and Products

### [The Lovable data exposure](https://www.crissyfield.de/research/lovable-en/): websites built without code, left without locks

A German security research team, working with Handelsblatt, analyzed 159,041 websites built with the Swedish AI app builder Lovable, whose pitch is that anyone can ship a database-backed web app in plain language. They found 24,664 sites returned database records the moment a page loaded, and probing further revealed email addresses mixed into roughly 30% of the tables examined. The deep cuts were alarming: a German lifestyle forum's 80,000-plus profiles and 200,000-plus chat messages, ticketing companies' purchase and payment records, and more than a thousand identity documents including passport numbers, all readable with no login. According to the report, Lovable's terms place data security on the customer, while its marketing leaves non-technical users believing the platform handles it, and those users are the least equipped to spot a misconfigured permission.

The thread ran hot. Some commenters raged at an industry happy to treat users as collateral damage in the AI race; others pushed back that this is the same old open-storage-bucket story and tools are blameless when misused. The middle ground's consensus pointed at secure defaults: ordinary people should be safe without needing expertise. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499017).

### [An old phone instead of a Pi](https://so.long.thanks.fish/why-pi-when-i-have-an-old-phone/): could the Redmi in your drawer save you a computer

One maker ran the numbers for a new installation: a used Redmi 13C or Huawei Mate 30 costs about 300 to 500 yuan secondhand and brings a 50-megapixel camera, GPS, battery, touchscreen, and 5G; matching that with a Raspberry Pi build, board plus camera, positioning, storage, battery, display, and a 4G module, runs over 2,000 yuan. The author has already unlocked and rooted the Redmi, wired it over USB-C to a custom light-controller board, and set up a photo rig to compare the phone against a dedicated camera module side by side, since a megapixel count alone settles nothing. The open question is scale: finding two good used phones is easy, but finding a few hundred with healthy batteries and consistent behavior is another matter.

The thread quickly became a reuse fair. Some readers gut broken-screen laptops for server boards, others recommend off-lease mini PCs, and a few warned that aging phone batteries and flash storage are the real hazards. The most upvoted debate asked what a Pi is actually for: interfaces or cheap convenience, since anyone who only needs a small server might be happier with a used mini PC. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50440709).

### [Terminal borders without the cost](https://www.joshka.net/cell-borders/): a little design sense for the black window

The maintainer of the Ratatui terminal-UI library has proposed a new border-drawing protocol. Classic terminal boxes use block characters that each swallow a whole cell on every side; the new approach has the terminal draw thin lines directly on cell edges, so tab labels, underlines beneath input fields, and rounded buttons stop eating into text space, and copied text no longer drags box-drawing cruft into chat. The author has patched the Ghostty terminal, written demos, and published the wire format, conflict-merging rules, and fallback behavior, with a long Q&A on edge cases. The closing pitch is a call for collaborators: this shouldn't stay one terminal's private feature.

Several commenters suggested the simpler overstrike mode could achieve much of the same, to which the author replied his design also covers colors, widths, and rounded corners. Another camp asked the bolder question: if the protocol is changing anyway, why not build a fully semantic UI layer instead. Defenders countered that the terminal's restraint is exactly why it has outlived its would-be replacements. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50491423).

## Policy and Governance

### [The EFF on tech's privacy rewrite](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/11/tech-companies-are-trying-shift-entire-concept-privacy-again): when your glasses remember the world for you

The Electronic Frontier Foundation argues that Meta and Google's smart glasses, the Apple Watch's audio transcription, and Amazon's always-listening Bee devices are turning continuous body-worn recording into the default future. Companies love touting technical safeguards such as on-device processing and face blurring, the article notes, but those protect the wearer, while bystanders, colleagues, and family get no real way to opt out. The law often treats such gadgets as walking microphones, the piece warns, and once the data exists, law enforcement will eventually come knocking; the deepest cost is social, as every casual chat becomes potentially archived and people grow reluctant to speak freely. The Foundation urges readers to file feedback with Apple and to voice discomfort politely but plainly when they encounter these devices.

Readers contrasted the present with the backlash that sank Google Glass a decade ago, debating whether young people are more tolerant or more wary. Office workers vented that every meeting now hosts an invisible AI guest, making even private one-on-ones feel overheard. Many conceded transcripts are genuinely useful, while warning that accuracy failures and legal exposure will detonate sooner or later. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50496602).

## Business and Platforms

### [An Nvidia director's $1B sale](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/nvidia-director-sold-nearly-1B-160000846.html): a timeline landing just before the scandal

Yahoo Finance reported that Nvidia board member Mark Stevens sold about 4.2 million shares in three batches from late August to mid-September, netting roughly $946 million, neatly positioned after a strong second-quarter earnings report and before CEO Jensen Huang's November 12 resignation. Filings show the sales did not use the standard pre-scheduled trading plans (arrangements that fix sale dates and prices in advance to guard against insider-trading suspicion), though Stevens still holds about 27.5 million shares. The resignation followed reports of Huang's extramarital affairs, with Colette Kress named interim CEO and the stock shedding about 15% within days. To be clear, the original reporting establishes a coincidence of timing, not a link between the sale and the resignation.

The thread split in two. One side called post-earnings-window selling with proper paperwork unremarkable; the other argued that a large sale without a pre-announced plan, landing just ahead of a scandal, should expect regulators to turn over every stone, whether or not anything was improper. Veterans of corporate compliance added that insider sales at large companies clear far more checkpoints than outsiders imagine. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50492993).

### [A thousand fake job ads taken down](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/linkedin-monster-take-down-over-1-000-fake-job-postings-after-cbc-investigation-9.7415379): high-paying remote roles hiding sign-up fees

A CBC undercover investigation found more than 1,500 remote travel-planner listings promising strong salaries with no experience required; applicants were funneled into online sessions for the US multi-level marketer Evolution Travel, where they were asked to pay hundreds of dollars upfront plus monthly fees, none of it hinted at in the ads. Reporters applying with blank resumes still received invitations, and traced one recruiter with a 300-person downline who denied the connection and hung up. After the story, LinkedIn removed about 900 listings and Monster about 600; a former FTC chair said such misleading pitches are exactly what the agency polices, noting the company had already received a warning back in 2021.

Comments widened from the case into estimates of the gray economy. Some readers suspected a large share of internet revenue is tinged with gray, with AI set to make scams harder to spot; job seekers lamented boards already full of ghost postings that go nowhere. The pragmatic advice: rely on referrals and direct applications, and treat public job boards as a backup. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499723).

### [What happened to 3Com](https://dfarq.homeip.net/what-happened-to-3com/): how the Ethernet inventor's company vanished

A blogger retraced 3Com's arc: founded in 1979 by Ethernet co-inventor Robert Metcalfe, it became the default network card of the 1990s, peaked at a $5.8 billion valuation during the dot-com boom, and even held the naming rights to San Francisco's Candlestick Park. The turn came from a string of self-inflicted wounds: walking away from the lucrative enterprise switch business and ceding it to Cisco, overpaying for modem and Palm maker US Robotics, and watching Intel undercut its network-card stronghold. Unprofitable every year from 2000 to 2005, its courtship with Huawei fell through, and HP bought the remains for $2.7 billion in 2009 before the brand faded. Today 3Com cards are prized by retro-computing hobbyists, still fondly remembered for working on the first try.

Former employees showed up with stories, including one about justifying a lifetime warranty with failure-rate math that cost the company almost nothing. Others linked the Computer History Museum's two-hour oral history with Metcalfe, alongside gentle mockery of his famous prediction that the internet would collapse in 1996. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50491830).

## Society and Culture

### [Monkey Island, 13 versions side by side](https://monkeyisland.pages.dev/): a playable exhibit of a 1990 pirate game

Two Italian brothers captured the same 12 scenes from all 13 releases of The Secret of Monkey Island frame by frame in emulators, building a page where versions can be dragged and compared: from 16-color and 256-color PC floppies through Amiga, Atari ST, Mac, and Sega CD, down to the wobble of the same signpost on different machines. The notes trace 1990 to 1993 changes, from a 12-verb interface cut to nine to music evolving from PC speaker beeps to CD audio, plus a lab of CRT display filters. The brothers say AI helped build the site and that they are remaking another 1986 adventure game in their spare time.

The thread turned into a nostalgia salon. Readers listed childhood classics and earnestly debated why early graphics cards looked so cold. The most upvoted recommendations were Return of the Obra Dinn and The Case of the Golden Idol, said to recapture the old puzzle-chewing joy. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50493904).

### [A family tree of software job titles](https://zwischenzugs.com/2026/11/03/a-family-tree-of-software-job-titles/): from typists to prompt engineers

One veteran engineer drew a family tree of software job titles: 104 titles from the 1940s to today, grouped into seven families covering development, architecture, operations, networking and security, data and AI, quality, and customer-facing roles, with solid lines for descent and dashed lines for influence. Six patterns emerged: coding began as low-status clerical work done largely by women; titles like webmaster split apart and rejoined; prompt engineer may fade as fast as Scrum Master; names like systems engineer get recycled for unrelated jobs; today's hottest field engineer is a remake of a 1960s practice; and the dev-versus-ops feud just keeps getting renamed. The author's parting joke: last time the typists turned out to be doing the hard part, so draw your own arrow this time.

The fiercest debate was whether DevOps is a culture or a job title. One side held it should be everyone's sense of ownership, and a dedicated post defeats the purpose; the other complained nobody loves on-call firefighting, and burnout incinerates exactly that ownership. Others mused on title inflation, where everyone wants the engineer name without clarifying what they can actually do. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50499038).

### [The night planes stopped and trains sold out](https://www.fox5dc.com/news/amtrak-outage-ticket-booking-faa-ground-stop-november-13-2026): Amtrak went down too

On Friday night, November 13, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ordered a nationwide ground stop over a telecommunications failure, the first since January 2023 and only the second since September 11, halting departures for over four hours. Meanwhile, travelers pivoting to rail found the booking site and app of Amtrak, the US national passenger railroad, throwing errors at scale, with outage trackers showing two evening complaint spikes and support lines too busy to answer. Amtrak said trains ran normally and blamed surging demand plus a system upgrade, with ticketing back around 9 p.m. The double disruption landed on a peak weekend travel window, and screenshots of failed checkouts flooded social media.

Comments became a global booking-system grievance session, spanning Spain, India, and Japan, where everyone has scars. The technically minded used the moment to argue public services must offer open booking interfaces instead of funneling everyone through one fragile website; longtime riders split sharply, with some calling the Amtrak app genuinely decent. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50492315).
