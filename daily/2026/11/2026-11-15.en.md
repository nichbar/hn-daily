# Today's Highlights

Who owns code written by a machine? A developer's provocation lit up the day's busiest thread: [since models grew up eating the open source community's cooking, AI-generated code should all be open source](https://jakub.jankiewicz.org/blog/ai-code-open-source/). Supporters cited the monkey-selfie copyright saga, skeptics asked where thoughtful guidance ends and lazy prompting begins, and the thread re-litigated three decades of free-software history.

Read together, today's conversations keep circling the same crack between technological promise and everyday cost. Memory prices have strained research budgets, graphics cards stand accused of engineered scarcity; an inbox outage silenced a pool alarm, a prettier screen leaves some viewers nauseous. On the brighter side, AI helped fix a thirty-year-old Jeep nobody else could diagnose, courts are asking whether police need a warrant to replay your driving history, crows decide twice as fast as humans, Sicilians mapped a thousand years of citrus onto one poster, and origin-of-life researchers stopped feuding to ask better questions. ## Policy and Governance

### Should AI-Grown Code Belong to Everyone

Developer Jakub Jankiewicz makes a blunt case: [all AI-generated code should be open source](https://jakub.jankiewicz.org/blog/ai-code-open-source/). His argument runs on two rails. First, today's models were trained on decades of open source code and Q&A posts, so turning that collective work into closed property looks like license laundering. Second, under the principle US copyright officials drew from the monkey-selfie dispute, non-human creations should not get copyright at all. To bridge jurisdictions such as the UK that grant limited rights to computer-generated works, he suggests developers proactively release machine-written code under open licenses or CC0 (a public-domain dedication), especially the write-a-prompt-and-ship-it kind produced with no real review. The original is a personal essay, not a legal verdict.

Note: the thread's most quoted voice was an AGPL (a license requiring derivatives to stay open) author who said a company cloned his system with a language model after a dispute, producing something clumsy yet unmistakably descended from his work. Many readers sympathized with that violated feeling, while top comments pushed back that carefully steered, hand-refined AI work should belong to its creator. The thread's main split came down to two practical knots: small developers cannot afford to litigate, and no court has settled where originality begins. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50507303)

### Is Replaying Your Driving History a Search

The EFF, joined by the ACLU (the American Civil Liberties Union), filed a friend-of-the-court brief asking the Sixth Circuit appeals court to treat searches of [automated license plate reader](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/11/were-asking-appeals-court-require-warrant-alpr-searches) (ALPR, cameras that photograph passing plates and log when and where each car appeared) databases as searches under the Constitution, requiring a warrant first. The case stems from Cleveland robberies where police located a suspect's car through years of commercial ALPR records without a warrant; the trial court held that plates in public view carry no privacy interest. The EFF counters that a passing roadside glance and a nationwide, years-deep movement archive are entirely different things, the latter being a time machine for anyone's past movements.

Note: several readers praised the New Hampshire model, check plates against a wanted list and delete everything else within minutes, as a working compromise. Others argued no retention rule can stop insider abuse and the cameras should never have entered neighborhoods at all. A middle camp proposed limiting use to kidnappings and homicides with short retention, a split that shows both the safety demand and the privacy dread are genuine. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50503255)

## Business and Platforms

### Memory Prices Nearly Triple, and a Fixing Suit Survives

A federal judge in Northern California refused to throw out a class action accusing Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron of conspiring to throttle conventional memory output while shifting capacity to AI-grade high-bandwidth memory, letting the [price-fixing suit](https://litnews.ai/articles/garciaguirre-samsung-sk-hynix-micron-dram-conspiracy-suit-survives-dismissal) proceed. The complaint says the trio controls over ninety percent of global memory revenue, conventional memory prices rose roughly 1.7 times in a year, yet nobody expanded capacity, alongside parallel moves such as long-term lock-ups, refused contracts, the closure of Micron's Crucial consumer brand, and delayed fabs. The court found these facts enough to infer conspiracy at the pleading stage, which is far from a conviction; the companies must answer by early December, with scheduling set for January.

Note: researchers in the thread vented that workstation memory had blown through grant budgets, forcing a rent-versus-build calculation for every project. Longtime readers recalled the same companies' earlier cartel convictions, though others cautioned that today's shortage follows an AI demand shock rather than the overcapacity glut behind the old scandal. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50502988)

### Nvidia Accused of Financing Its Own Demand to Hold GPU Prices Up

TechSpot, summarizing a Wall Street Journal profile of Nvidia's interim CEO, reports the chipmaker used [circular financing](https://www.techspot.com/news/114558-nvidia-deliberately-kept-gpu-prices-high-through-circular.html) (funding customers to buy its own chips, creating an illusion of demand) to absorb excess TSMC wafer orders and keep graphics card prices high. The report says executives already worried the deals had grown too large, while the flagship RTX 5090 became nearly unobtainable as memory was steered toward data-center cards, pushing street prices toward 160 percent of list. The backdrop is former CEO Jensen Huang's resignation amid personal scandal and chip-diversion allegations; the claims remain single-sourced reporting, not established fact.

Note: the thread's liveliest what-if asked whether a $2,000 flagship card would put capable AI in every home. Optimists argued local open models would end subscriptions, with one hobbyist reporting a new open model runs fine on a modest home rig. Skeptics countered that electricity, tinkering time, and phone-app convenience mean most people will keep paying monthly, since the two lifestyles price out very differently. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50501631)

## Tech and Products

### A More Beautiful MacBook Screen That Makes Some People Dizzy

A MacAnorak long read reports the new MacBook Pro's [PROLED tandem OLED display](https://macanorak.com/the-macbook-pros-oled-pwm-problem-when-a-better-display-becomes-worse-to-use/) stuns on contrast and brightness yet leaves some users with headaches, nausea, and lost focus. Lab testing measured flicker at 240Hz against nearly 15,000Hz on the previous Mini-LED model, a sixty-fold gap, with some owners returning machines within a week. The author stresses this is not an OLED curse but a dimming-design choice: PWM (pulse-width modulation, a technique that controls brightness by switching light rapidly on and off) implementations differ enormously between panels. Apple once added a flicker-softening accessibility option on iPhones, and the piece asks for similar user control on the Mac, advising anyone sensitive to try before buying with a full workday test.

Note: the comments became a support group for the flicker-sensitive, with readers sharing returned monitors and phone slow-motion tricks for spotting bad lighting. The consensus held that sufferers likely far outnumber posters, since many people endure eye strain for years without suspecting the screen, and vendors should treat display comfort as an accessibility issue. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50506719)

### One Undelivered Email, 15 People Gassed by Chlorine

German broadcaster WDR reports that a faulty auto-dosing system pumped excess chlorine into a Phantasialand hotel pool near Cologne, while the maintenance alarm could only send email. [The alert went nowhere during a major Microsoft 365 outage](https://www1.wdr.de/nrw/rheinland/rhein-erft-kreis/technischer-defekt-chlor-phantasialand-100.html), fifteen guests reported symptoms, and the pool water was replaced afterward. The report, citing the park, traces the direct cause to defective dosing equipment, with the missing email cutting the last recovery link; injuries were minor and the pool has reopened.

Note: commenters near-unanimously called email-only safety alerting a design sin, arguing for on-site sirens plus human rounds, since email by design guarantees nothing about delivery. Debate centered on blame, the hotel for buying a cheap setup versus the vendor for offering email as the only channel, but most agreed the real lesson is that critical systems must assume every external dependency will fail. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509411)

### AI, a $3 Board, and a 32-Year-Old Jeep Brought Back to Life

A YouTuber's aging Jeep failed emissions, and no shop could talk to its 1994 engine computer. With a three-dollar Arduino (a hobbyist microcontroller board for hardware experiments) wired in, [Claude and Codex puzzled through undocumented wiring](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WPj3aQ-xxpI), cracked a protocol with no surviving manual, and eventually read live sensor data, logged drives, and even drove the fuel pump to confirm a diagnosis. The draw is not car repair itself but the pattern: a model chaining half-forgotten hardware lore, protocol guesses, and hands-on experiments into a path an ordinary person can follow.

Note: judging by the video chapters and thread heat, readers were most excited to see AI cross from pure software into the physical world, with one calling it reverse engineering for everyone. Commenters also cautioned that an old car's forgiving protocol invites tinkering in ways brakes or airbags never should, so sort out the cost of being wrong before touching anything. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50463399)

## Science and Research

### Crows Decide Twice as Fast as Humans Can

The New York Times covers a Science journal study in which two carrion crows responded to visual cues at twice the speed of humans and monkeys, the [first quantified measurement of avian decision-making speed](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/15/science/birdbrain-crows-birds-research-decision.html). Once dismissed as simple-minded for their small brains, crows have spent two decades overturning the verdict: corvids use tools, count, recognize themselves, and read the intentions of their peers. An uninvolved comparative psychologist quoted in the piece put it plainly: birds are simply faster than monkeys or us, and impressively so.

Note: the thread turned into a semantics debate, with many arguing birdbrain always meant stupid rather than slow, so speed alone proves little about intelligence. The most upvoted reading held that the study's real value is showing how efficient compact bird brains are. Resident crow-watchers chimed in with tales of cooperative owl-chasing and windy-day aerobatics that looked suspiciously like showing off. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50509023)

### After Fifty Years of Feuding, Origin-of-Life Science Tries New Questions

A Quanta Magazine feature traces a shift in origin-of-life research: after decades of warring over whether RNA, lipids, proteins, or clay came first, some researchers called the field gridlocked. [A new generation has stopped insisting on a single starter molecule](https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-evolving-story-of-how-chemistry-became-biology-20261112/), mixing strange chemical soups forward from the nonliving world, reasoning backward from living genetics, and probing never-existing life with synthetic biology. From the 1952 Miller-Urey flask experiment to today, the piece argues there is no single equation for life's start; the hard question is how molecular processes became a system that evolves stably.

Note: a physics-trained reader said the strategic pivot finally made sense, since every single-track bet had stalled, and finding a field stuck enough to change tactics felt oddly familiar. Skepticism focused on claims pushing life's start off Earth, while the longest side thread argued over defining life itself, from self-creation to entropy gradients, with philosophical density running high. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50482222)

### A Forty-Year-Old Console Still Gets New Games

Blogger Jordi rounds up [25 favorite modern homebrew games for the NES](https://jordi.inversethought.com/blog/25-of-my-favourite-modern-nes-games/), the 8-bit console born in 1983 that still attracts fresh releases. The list is a springboard; the comments became a nostalgia convention, with readers showing off Eastern Europe's nineties clones with names like Gold Leopard King and Terminator 2, recalling blind flea-market cartridge buys on months of pocket money, and Spanish readers inventorying bootleg multi-game carts. As an aside, parts of the homebrew scene now bar quick AI-generated entries, insisting on hand-crafted work.

Note: the liveliest corner was multinational clone archaeology, from Romania's Russian markets to Spain's ubiquitous N-in-1 carts, with readers cross-checking cartridge formats and compatibility. Working developers described the full pipeline from no-code tools to pressed physical cartridges, nostalgia wrapped around genuine hands-on fun. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50503465)

### Sicilians Drew a Thousand Years of Citrus as One Family Tree

Travel writer Erik Gauger hand-drew a poster mapping [a millennium of Sicilian citrus](https://www.notesfromtheroad.com/europa/citrus-family-tree.html), starting from three wild Asian ancestors, citron, pomelo, and papeda (a wild citrus relative). Sour oranges, sweet oranges, lemons, and clementines all descend through centuries of crossing and bud mutation, while Etna's three blood oranges trace to a single red mutation. Each fruit's story is pinned to one of eight Sicilian travel stops, with genome-study citations attached, so citrus lovers can literally tour the tree.

Note: the comments gathered citrus obsessives, with questions about where Australia's finger limes fit and tales of pandemic balcony seedlings still years from fruit. The longest exchange debated whether the clementine is a hybrid or a mutation, and the author showed up citing a 2014 genome study to settle it, in full field-guide spirit. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50448895)
