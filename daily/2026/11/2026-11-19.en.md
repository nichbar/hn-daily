# Today's Highlights

The liveliest thread of the day started with a [garage door](https://blog.harrison.page/garage-door-spam/): a homeowner found marketing messages inside what should have been plain safety alerts, and ripped out the cloud in favor of a small open-source board. That little revolt sets the tone for the whole day. Control is slipping away from ordinary people, and people are grabbing it back. The same day brought a British tribunal forcing the government to admit a secret order against Apple's encryption, a safety filter blocking an engineer from fixing his own product's vulnerability, and Anthropic pricing its public listing amid a fierce argument over what AI companies are actually worth. Softer news came from science: a virus that stopped pancreatic tumors in three patients, and an AI that cracked two long-open theory problems about black holes.

## Tech and Products

### Ads inside the garage door

A homeowner's [escape story](https://blog.harrison.page/garage-door-spam/) describes how the myQ app, once a simple channel for open-and-close alerts, started injecting marketing messages into what should be pure safety notifications. The author explains that the manufacturer cut off third-party access years ago, so he bought a roughly $62 open-source board, the ratgdo32, and wired it to talk to the opener over a local protocol, cutting the vendor cloud out entirely. Top comments shared the same frustration, with readers naming bank emails, TV home screens, and even a fan-control app as offenders. Several readers pushed back with a principle: charging money and showing ads should be mutually exclusive, or operating systems should force marketing alerts into a separate channel with a global off switch. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50519231)

### A safety filter that blocked a security fix

An Estonian engineer's [blog post](https://ounapuu.ee/posts/2026/11/19/unsafeguards/) vents about trying to use Claude Code to patch a vulnerability in his own product, only to be stopped by the model's safety filter. Unlocking it meant waiting days for approval, giving up data protections, and uploading an ID. The irony, the author writes, is that real attackers using unfiltered open models face none of this friction, and he ended up verifying and fixing the flaw with an EU-hosted open model instead. Commenters widely agreed, saying Claude now refuses tasks it handled fine a month ago and that they are moving toward local or open models. Others argued the filters look less like safety and more like a regulatory moat being built ahead of the IPO. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562262)

### A Java fix that sat unread for two months

Back in September 2026, someone submitted a [fix](https://github.com/openjdk/jdk/pull/32781) for an out-of-bounds write in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder (the component that decodes a widely used data encoding). It sat unreviewed, nearly got auto-closed, and the submitter closed it themselves. Two months later the flaw blew up as a major vulnerability dubbed java4shell. The submission itself is just code, and the story is all in the timeline. A commenter claiming JDK experience explained that backend crashes get routed into a silent security review, so public silence does not always mean nothing happened. Still, the thread's main split was over triage at scale: with AI-generated submissions flooding in, even a report with a ready fix can vanish, and some suggested automated model-assisted triage. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50558906)

## Society and Culture

### Held together by people who give a damn

An essay titled [We Need Smart People Who Give a Shit](https://violetpixel.com/blog/2026-11-17-we-need-smart-people-who-give-a-shit/) drew the day's deepest agreement. Using bank transfer systems as an example, the author argues that fragile infrastructure keeps running only because people who know it intimately keep patching it by hand. Since generative AI arrived, managers mistake output for understanding, lay people off or turn them into model babysitters, and the remaining staff lose both the knowledge and the attachment that came from building things. The thread echoed the diagnosis, with readers saying that caring at work gets punished while storytelling gets promoted. Several readers pushed back gently, noting the rot starts at the top and advising people to protect their own wellbeing first. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50554045)

### A civilization reboot kit for your laptop

[Lastbook](https://lastbook.org/) is a free, local, offline knowledge pack for computers: a local AI assistant, more than 258,000 searchable places, over 2,300 videos, and illustrated guides for water, first aid, food, and power, all usable after one download. The project's site lists a 7.7 GB minimal install and a roughly 388 GB full library, with no phone support. The thread's most practical voices warned that hoarding files is not the same as having skills: survival knowledge only counts if practiced until it is familiar. Other readers confessed, with humor, that no number of videos could teach their hands, setting off a warm exchange about practice and failure. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50560822)

## Science and Research

### A virus that stopped pancreatic tumors

[New Scientist reports](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2528235-pancreatic-cancer-halted-by-virus-injection-in-three-patients/) that a cancer-killing virus developed at the University of Minnesota stopped pancreatic tumors from growing and spreading in three people in an early US safety trial. Notably, the doses were only one-tenth of the eventual target, a result the lead researcher said beat his expectations. Pancreatic cancer is notoriously hard to catch early and quick to advance, which is why even a small signal draws attention. The report stresses this is a tiny early sample needing much larger trials. The comments filled with personal stories of parents lost within months of diagnosis after repeated misdiagnoses, and a doctor explained why early biopsy is itself so difficult, leaving the thread holding a cautious hope. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50563066)

### Claude cracks two old black-hole problems

Anthropic's [research blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-black-hole-physics) says Claude solved two long-open theory problems about black holes and quantum gravity: it completed a 2003 calculation on how hot gas snaps into a black hole, showing the abrupt transition survives even at the quantum level, and it pinned down the exact ceiling for the simplest version of the bootstrap method (an approach that narrows the search by ruling out inconsistent theories). The process reads like a story in itself, with teams of Claude agents working in parallel for about six weeks and the proofs machine-checked with the Lean proof-checking software. The post is candid that neither result describes the real universe or has practical uses. Commenters stayed measured, with one noting that dozens of comments barely discussed the papers at all, and others insisting that method details and independent replication matter far more than headlines. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50563148)

## Policy and Governance

### London forced to admit the secret encryption order

The independent blog Macanorak [reports](https://macanorak.com/uk-government-forced-to-admit-it-issued-apple-a-secret-encryption-order/) that a UK tribunal ruled the government must acknowledge it served Apple with a secret Technical Capability Notice demanding access to encrypted iCloud data. The saga traces to an exposed global order in early 2025, which Apple answered by pulling its end-to-end encryption feature for new UK users rather than complying; after diplomacy killed the first order, a second one reportedly limited to UK users followed. The ruling only lifts the secrecy over the order's existence, with the substance and legality left for a hearing expected in 2027. The thread centered on trust: commenters noted Apple's wording quietly shifted from nobody-can-read to Apple-cannot-read, a distinction most users would miss, while others argued Apple is resisting under compulsion and the real problem is a secrecy regime users cannot even see. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50562113)

## Business and Platforms

### Anthropic prices its public debut

Anthropic [announced](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering) it priced Class A shares at $83, offering nearly 980 million shares for trading on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. It is the landmark moment of AI labs meeting the public market: the company must explain heavy losses and growth to retail investors, and its reception will set the benchmark for whoever lists next. The thread split sharply, with skeptics calling Anthropic the AltaVista of the AI era and predicting a dot-com-scale reckoning at trillion-dollar valuations. Defenders countered with the company's tens of billions in 2026 revenue, arguing real demand makes the bubble comparison too simple. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50553843)

### When will storage get cheap again

Storage company DriveVault's [blog](https://www.drivevault.io/blog/when-will-hard-drive-prices-come-down.html) combines analyst forecasts with vendor earnings calls into a timeline: SSD prices should ease in the second half of 2027 as flash supply catches up, in a slow slide rather than a crash, while spinning drives stay tight into 2028 because factory capacity is already promised to data centers. The post warns the low prices of a few years ago are not coming back soon: buy now if you need storage for work, wait on SSDs if you are speculating, and auditing old drives for duplicates is the cheapest upgrade. Commenters added a financial angle, arguing that single-digit earnings multiples on memory stocks show the market already bets on a profit collapse, with the only debate being how fast prices fall. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50557716)
