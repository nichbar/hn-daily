# Today's Highlights

The day's biggest story came from [Nvidia's boardroom announcement](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html): Jensen Huang is back as president, CEO, and director, and the board openly said the decision followed input from senior levels of the US government. When the world's most valuable chip company needs a nod from Washington to pick its chief executive, that says something about where corporate power really sits. Around that headline, a common thread ran through the day: independence and self-reliance. An indie browser reached usable alpha, an open-source mail server stepped in for aging Exchange boxes, and homebrew AI moved into the living room. Friction over who sets the rules was just as visible, from a chip reference site's shutdown to an app's EU delisting.

## Business and Platforms

### Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang

[Nvidia's board](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html) announced that Jensen Huang has been reinstated as president, CEO, and board director with immediate effect. The board said that after extended discussions and input from senior US government officials, it was satisfied his return posed no national security risk. Huang had been sidelined after reports that he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and an independent investigation into the matter will continue. The board also created a national security and export-compliance committee and will hire a chief compliance officer, pledging cooperation with ongoing government inquiries.

Note: the thread's most endorsed reading paired this reversal with the OpenAI ouster saga, arguing both episodes show nominal board authority collapsing when heavier forces intervene, which casts doubt on governance schemes that have never been stress-tested. Other commenters debated whether the relief rally priced out misconduct risk or priced in a government backstop. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50611576).

### An Open-Source Heir to Exchange

[Stalwart](https://stalw.art/blog/microsoft-exchange-replacement/) released a new version that speaks Microsoft Exchange protocols fluently: Outlook, Apple Mail, and phone mail apps connect as if to an Exchange server, with mail, calendars, contacts, tasks, and notes in one account. The timing matters. Older Exchange Server versions are out of support, the subscription edition gets no major new features, and the new Outlook for Windows does not support on-premises accounts at all. Stalwart ships the Exchange support in its open-source community edition, catching organizations that want their mailboxes to stay in their own server rooms.

Note: veteran self-hosters showed up with war stories about spam filtering, sync quirks, and calendaring edge cases, warning newcomers to budget real operations effort. Supporters countered that with Microsoft herding everyone toward its cloud, one more mature open option is significant in itself. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50620349).

### Nespresso Quietly Trims Its Capsules

A coffee blogger exposed an internal [Nespresso "Project Olympus" plan](https://alvinbunk.wordpress.com/2026/11/01/nespresso-project-olympus/): starting in 2026, many Vertuo capsule recipes would be quietly lightened under the banner of grinding and roasting optimizations, with taste and caffeine claimed unchanged. The post reproduces internal memos and approved Q&A lines coaching staff on how to answer customers who notice lighter capsules. The author says many drinkers are threatening to defect to fresh-ground setups and keeps a spreadsheet tracking the shrinkage across flavors.

Note: the thread quickly became a coffee-status arena, with converts from pods to grinders testifying that fresh-ground wins outright while holdouts defended one-button convenience. Several readers sighed that shrink-without-discounting has become this generation's standard consumer-goods script. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50605479).

## Tech and Products

### Indie Browser Ladybird Reaches Alpha

The independent browser [Ladybird](https://ladybird.org/posts/alpha/) is now in alpha, with downloads for macOS and Linux and rolling automatic updates. Its headline feature is a rendering engine, the core component that turns web code into the pages you see, built from scratch rather than yet another Chromium fork, at a time when nearly every browser shares Chromium DNA. The team is candid that the alpha will break sites and crash sometimes, invites bug reports from inside the browser, welcomes security researchers to take their best shot, and asks web developers to test their own sites for compatibility.

Note: commenters praised the pragmatic pivot to battle-tested components for speed, saying it gets a usable product out faster, while others felt nostalgic for the early hand-rolled-everything hacker spirit. A lively sub-thread debated the project's embrace of AI-assisted development and the copyright questions around it. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50614249).

### A Chinese Lab Bets on Loop Transformers

Zhipu released its flagship [GLM-5.4](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.4) model, the first built on a Loop Transformer architecture: one shared block of layers runs for multiple iterations so the model reasons more deeply without adding parameters. The company claims intelligence comparable to models with two to three trillion parameters and frames the release as the start of "generalized coding," where code becomes the foundation for solving a much wider range of tasks. It also admits a flaw: the model falls into marathon thinking loops, though that prolonged self-correction does keep lifting accuracy.

Note: users in the thread griped that marathon thinking makes simple edits take far longer than rival models, with several calling it the predictable result of rewarding only pass-or-fail outcomes. Others pointed out that closed models ramble just as much but hide their reasoning traces, so users never notice. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50609685).

### A Beginner's Guide to Homebrew AI

A popular guide walks readers through [building a free household chat AI](https://www.gpupartner.com/blog/beginners-guide-local-ai-inkling-qwen-unsloth) from open-weight models, AI models anyone may download and run privately, on a home PC. The author's math: cloud models charge by the token, wobble in quality, impose content limits, and raise privacy questions, so offloading research gathering and coding chores to a local model on a good graphics card means one upfront cost and unlimited use for the whole family.

Note: several commenters called this the plasma-TV phase of local AI, bulky and pricey but clearly the direction of travel, with one estimating most practical AI runs at home within about five years. Skeptics invoked the bitter lesson to argue datacenter giants will stay permanently ahead, sparking a good-enough-versus-strongest tug of war. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603845).

## Policy and Governance

### Yann LeCun Takes Aim at AI Regulation Rhetoric

AI pioneer Yann LeCun [publicly accused a rival lab's chief executive](https://twitter.com/ylecun/status/2124550037912920090) of hyping AI dangers to get open-source models regulated away and cement his own control. The post dragged a long-simmering split into the open: one camp says only strict controls can avert catastrophe, the other says safety talk is a commercial moat in disguise. It became the day's most heated policy brawl.

Note: the thread split hard. One side reported that regulatory circles already float ideas like throttling consumer graphics cards for model workloads or registering private compute, arguing open weights cannot be recalled once released. The other side questioned the safety camp's motives, noting its funding ties to big labs, while a middle faction proposed policing datacenters and leaving personal experimentation alone. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50615308).

### Arm Forces a Beloved Reference Site Offline

The engineer-favorite [Arm instruction lookup site](https://arm.jonpalmisc.com/) posted a farewell notice: at Arm's request, it goes dark on December 4. The maintainer says Arm's lawyers cited trademark and documentation-hosting claims. The lightweight, searchable site had become a beloved stand-in for the official docs with their popups and bloat, and the maintainer says he is exploring mirrors and open-source generator scripts to keep bookmarks alive.

Note: the maintainer himself showed up to explain that working around trademark-plus-hosted-docs claims is genuinely hard. Commenters overwhelmingly called the move self-defeating for Arm, suggesting an acquisition or a hire would have been smarter, while others proposed proxy shells that pull from official sources or anonymous torrent distributions. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50617499).

### Apple Boots a Nine-Year-Old Open-Source App from EU Stores

An independent developer says his nine-year-old [open-source notes app](https://github.com/glushchenko/fsnotes/discussions/2056) vanished from every EU App Store with appeals going nowhere, starting from a missing October payout. Thread detectives dug up a cached Dutch store page showing the app lacked the trader verification the EU's Digital Services Act requires, pointing to a compliance removal rather than malice. The author later confirmed he was stuck in paperwork and admitted using AI help to polish the post.

Note: the discussion centered on compliance costs for solo developers, with the law's demand to publish a home address and phone number feeling disproportionate for free open-source work, though some insisted rules are rules. A parallel chorus blasted Apple's distant, unresponsive support, finding big-company service worse than tiny shops'. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50616933).

## Science and Research

### A Medieval Cathedral Surfaces in Sudan

Archaeologists in northern Sudan have uncovered [the original floor of a monumental medieval cathedral](https://apnews.com/article/sudan-antiquities-nubia-cathedral-1b16f67da881ee5592de4481ee55c527), possibly larger than the White House and among the most important Christian buildings of medieval Nubia. Buried under sand for centuries, its scale and stonework now offer fresh physical evidence for the Christian chapter of Nile-valley civilization. Field footage shows floor slabs and column bases seeing daylight after a millennium.

Note: comments drifted quickly from archaeology to identity politics, with one reader lamenting the near-invisibility of non-Arab peoples in Western coverage, which drew both "why should distant readers care" pushback and "suppressed minorities deserve global reporting" support. Others compared Nubia's coverage with hotter conflict zones. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50547698).

### A Second Orbiter Finally Reaches Mercury

After eight years and six billion kilometers, the joint European-Japanese [BepiColombo probe](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/20/spacecraft-mercury-second-ever-mission-orbit-bepicolombo) fired its thrusters into orbit around Mercury, only the second mission in history to circle the innermost planet. Because the Sun's gravity constantly accelerates inbound craft, the probe spent years braking to avoid overshooting. Two orbiters will separate in December, one mapping surface chemistry and one sensing the magnetic field and the water ice hiding in permanently shadowed craters, with science operations starting next spring.

Note: the thread's favorite bit was orbital-mechanics trivia, with veterans explaining that reaching Mercury costs more fuel than leaving the Solar System and required multiple Venus and Earth flybys just to slow down. When someone asked whether a private company could send its own probe, old hands walked through the physics step by step. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50587439).

### A 940-Page Handwritten Bid for the Riemann Hypothesis

Mathematician John Baez published a long explainer unpacking [Alessandro Masullo's 940-page Riemann Hypothesis manuscript](https://johncarlosbaez.wordpress.com/2026/11/22/masullo-on-the-riemann-hypothesis/), sketching its strategy around a transformed view of the zeta function's zeros and flagging problems specialists have already raised. Meanwhile a separate AI-generated proof running to tens of millions of lines of machine-checked code still awaits human verdict. Baez stresses he is no number theorist and invites corrections, framing the duel as human mathematics versus machine mathematics head-on.

Note: sympathy for Masullo dominated, with commenters calling twelve years of work colliding with a machine-proof flood heartbreaking and criticizing AI labs for announcing results without releasing models. Cooler heads noted priority collisions are routine in mathematics and that early release may earn the author hundreds of expert reviewers, saving years of dead ends. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50598441).

## Society and Culture

### The Internet Archive Grows Its Own TV Network

[Spelunkr](https://spelunkr.tv/) calls itself a free alternative TV network built entirely from the Internet Archive's forgotten videos: open the site and flip between unfamiliar channels of decades-old ads, shows, and home footage. The project streams straight from the Archive without commercialization. Commenters dug up the Archive's early blessing of hotlinking, arguing this reuse is exactly its mission, with several urging donations to keep the Archive alive.

Note: reactions to the concept split between "just remixing other people's work" dismissal and defenders citing a decade of similar good-citizen projects as precedent. A side debate asked what TV is even for: some wanted only news-grade topicality, others said old news with "here is what happened next" footnotes would be genuinely valuable. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50606192).

### Who Are You Writing That Blog Post For?

A modest [essay on writing advice](https://danielsada.tech/blog/assume-people-are-not-interested-in-your-blog-post/) unexpectedly blew up: its author argues you should assume nobody cares about your post and say upfront who it is for. Critics replied that blogs exist to be written for oneself and shared along the way, and that pandering to readers rings false. Supporters countered that even writing for your future self means holding an audience in mind, or the piece will baffle even you later.

Note: the thread leaned heavily toward writing for yourself, with longtime bloggers describing journals kept for clarity rather than clicks and defining success as the act of finishing. A mediating faction drew a line between writing and publishing: once public, the piece enters a transaction with the world, and at minimum the title and opening owe readers an account. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50603143).
