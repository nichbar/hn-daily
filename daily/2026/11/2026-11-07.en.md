# Today's Highlights

The most-upvoted story today is about a library most people have never heard of: the author of the open-source terminal app Ghostty has spun its core engine into a stable C library and released version 1.0. Over the past year it has quietly been adopted by development tools used by tens of millions of people. Also on the front page: a fan project that brings a classic 2004 racing game into the browser, the behind-the-scenes story of Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group, and lively debates about Apple's on-device knowledge graph, algorithmic rent pricing, ancient DNA, and robot factories.

## Tech and Products

### Libghostty 1.0

Ghostty's author, Mitchell Hashimoto, announced that the terminal emulation core extracted from his open-source app, [libghostty-vt](https://mitchellh.com/writing/libghostty-1-0), has reached 1.0, meaning the C API and its binary interface are now stable. Over the last year the library has quietly become plumbing for the industry: JetBrains' IDEs swapped their terminals to it (over 12.5 million active users), Neovim plans to adopt it, and KDE, Vercel, and dozens of new tools build on it. The 1.0 milestone matters because it promises strict interface compatibility, so packagers and developers can depend on it without fear of breakage.

The thread's commentators appreciated that the author listed adopters without plugging his own competing product, and several said terminal emulation is a thankless problem best consolidated into a few high-quality implementations. That contrast between the author's own framing and the HN read is the interesting part.

### Need for Speed Underground 2 online in the browser

A developer recompiled the game (converting the original machine code into code that runs in a browser tab) and brought it to [nfsu2.xyz](https://nfsu2.xyz/), partly to relive childhood sessions with his brother. It supports free roam, online races, cloud saves, and up to 120 fps gameplay, with roughly 8,000 players online at a time. The project is free, has no microtransactions, and is funded by donations, with source code on GitHub.

HN users shared warm memories of playing the game on friends' PCs and marveled at running it on a phone today. Several wished more abandoned classics could be revived this way — one commenter imagined a SimCity where neighboring cities are run by real players — while others noted the legal gray area of reverse-engineering copyright-abandoned titles.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50403052)

### Claude Code's "Don't Ask Again" exposes passwords in 1,122 public GitHub files

Security firm Apiiro found that the AI coding assistant [Claude Code](https://apiiro.com/blog/coding-agents-leaking-secrets-github-dont-ask-again) saves the commands a developer approves, verbatim. Click "yes, don't ask again" at a permission prompt and a command containing a password, cloud key, or API token gets written to a settings file in the project — which often ends up committed to GitHub. Scanning over 100,000 public copies of the file, the researchers found 1,122 holding real secrets across 63 organizations, many of them database passwords or production tokens. Their advice: rotate credentials now, and turn on secret scanning.

A Claude Code team member responded in the thread that the file is meant to stay local and is auto-gitignored, recommending the safer "auto" mode and secret scanners. Other developers pushed back that the design of permanently recording approved commands is itself the problem, and that tools should default to scrubbing sensitive values.

## Society and Culture

### Every Mac comes with an (undocumented) family member classifier

Security researchers disclosed that every Mac shipped since 2022 carries a pair of undocumented background daemons that sift through Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, location, and screen time to build a "personal knowledge graph" about you and your social circle — inferring relationships such as kinship and employment. The team released an open-source dashboard, [iKnow](https://8rank.ai/game/apple-intelligence-personal-knowledge-graph), so users can inspect what the graph holds, and explored how such data could be abused if it fell into the wrong hands.

Several HN commenters called the discovery "creepy but unsurprising." Even if data stays on-device, they argued, silently analyzing your relationships changes how people interact — if a machine remembers every detail, forgetting a colleague's new nephew becomes unacceptable. Others noted this kind of on-device intelligence is widespread, and the real issue is transparency and control.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50403097)

### Upload Your RAM

[UploadYourRam.org](https://uploadyourram.org/) is a deadpan parody of the idea that RAM in personal computers is wasteful: visitors can "donate" idle memory to large language models and receive a thank-you from an AI CEO's generated likeness. The joke landed, and the thread turned into a real debate about whether centralized AI is turning personal computers back into thin clients of an earlier mainframe era.

HN commentators split into two camps: some recalled how software-as-a-service already downgraded the PC and see AI repeating the playbook, while others believe cheap local models will eventually return agency to individuals — though rising hardware costs are currently discouraging that community.

### Notes on San Marino

Travel writer Matt Lakeman spent three days in San Marino, the self-proclaimed world's oldest republic, and filed [Notes on San Marino](https://mattlakeman.org/2026/11/05/notes-on-san-marino/) with his trademark deadpan humor. The microstate, fully enclosed by Italy, has about 33,500 residents, is slightly larger than Manhattan, and has a murder rate of zero. Lakeman recounts 13 moments when the country nearly got swallowed by popes, Borgias, and Napoleon, concluding that smallness, diplomatic agility, and sheer luck let it outlast every wave of European consolidation.

HN readers appreciated the line that "boring is good," compared the country's functioning government to Switzerland's, and mused about what makes a tiny state economically sustainable in the modern world.

## Business and Platforms

### The end of Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group

Machine-learning researcher John Langford wrote that Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group, which he co-founded, has effectively been dissolved after being folded into the broader Microsoft AI division. In [his farewell post](https://hunch.net/?p=13764006), he defends the group's work on agent frameworks and fundamental research, and criticizes the new organization's "scaling-only" philosophy — an obsession with bigger models and more data at the expense of exploratory research. He has left Microsoft, as have other founding members.

Most HN commenters sided with the value of research, arguing that betting everything on scale sacrifices long-term innovation. A vocal minority pushed back, noting that scaling laws haven't hit a ceiling yet and that doubling down on size is the rational play right now. The thread is a genuine window into the industry's biggest strategic argument.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50406563)

### I toured Unitree's factory. I'm more bullish on the West

A factory tour of the Chinese robotics firm Unitree revealed that its Hangzhou plant assembles robots mostly by hand: the company's machinery is worth about $1.7 million on the books, yet it made nearly 32,000 robots last year. In [the write-up](https://josef.cn/blog/unitree), the author calculates that labor is only 8% of manufacturing cost, so automation saves little — and fast product iteration makes dedicated lines obsolete quickly. In America, he argues, higher wages make automation far more profitable, and new rules requiring domestically made parts give Western automation vendors the edge.

The thread split sharply: some readers said the conclusion overreaches and that China's automation track record is formidable, while others agreed that expensive labor is what forces the machine-building machines, citing the history of factory automation from Ford's line to tomato harvesters.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50400597)

## Science and Research

### A barbarian tribe's mythic origin story is true, ancient DNA reveals

Science magazine reports in [“A barbarian tribe's mythic origin story is true, ancient DNA reveals”](https://www.science.org/content/article/barbarian-tribe-s-mythic-origin-story-true-ancient-dna-reveals) that ancient DNA has for the first time backed a legend: the Gothic tribes that harried the Roman Empire did originate in Scandinavia, and a mass migration carried them to what is now northern Poland. The findings challenge decades of scholarship that downplayed population movement in favor of cultural diffusion, and one historian not involved in the work said the genetics show ancient written sources were more reliable than twentieth-century historiography admitted.

HN commenters explained the academic context — the postwar reaction against Nazi-era archaeology made scholars wary of migration narratives — and cautioned against equating myth with genetics. The later spread of Gothic culture, they noted, may still have been more cultural than demographic, and only more samples will tell.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50401740)

### Greece has its own Pompeii: Thera buried Akrotiri in ash 17th-16th century BCE

Smithsonian Magazine tells the story of Akrotiri in [“Greece Has Its Own Pompeii”](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/greece-has-its-own-pompeii-and-the-ruins-tell-a-captivating-story-of-a-sophisticated-seafaring-bronze-age-society-180989747/), a Bronze Age port city on Santorini buried under roughly 200 feet of ash when the Thera volcano erupted around 3,600 years ago — an eruption about four times as powerful as Krakatoa's in 1883. Unlike Pompeii, Akrotiri's residents appear to have escaped by boat; no victims have been found. The site reveals a sophisticated seafaring society with multi-story buildings, frescoes, and drainage systems, though only about 10% has been excavated.

HN readers marveled at the uniqueness of the Minoan frescoes, debated recent claims of deciphering the still-unread Linear A script, and traded travel tips — urging visitors to see the actual ruins rather than just the famous blue-domed roofs.

Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50360810)

### How Long Can Life Wait?

This [science essay](https://mceglowski.substack.com/p/how-long-can-life-wait) asks how long organisms can stay dormant and still revive. Ultraviolet light is the main killer of microbes in space, but a thin layer of dust shields them — bacteria on the International Space Station survived three years in orbit. That has implications for planetary protection, for the theory that life traveled between planets inside rocks, and for the contested claims of reviving hundreds-of-millions-of-years-old microbes from salt deposits.

The thread mixed the philosophical and the technical: one reader reflected on mortality and urged people to cherish human connections, while others dug into the literature, noting that the sterilization protocols behind the oldest "revived" bacteria claims have credible holes.

## Policy and Governance

### RealPage rent-pricing case wraps with nearly $360M to renters

A federal court in Tennessee approved a class-action settlement that will send nearly $360 million to US renters who lived in properties using RealPage's revenue-management software between 2018 and 2025. The plaintiffs alleged that [RealPage's algorithm](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/realpage-rent-pricing-case-360m-renters/873553/) let landlords share non-public data and coordinate rent increases; the defendants did not admit fault but agreed to stop feeding non-public data into the pricing system. Legal experts caution that whether the decree actually curbs "algorithmic collusion" remains to be seen.

HN commenters used the case to call for a public registry of rental prices, arguing that symmetric information is the real fix. Several were also skeptical of the settlement size relative to total overcharges, and said regulators should keep tightening algorithmic pricing.
