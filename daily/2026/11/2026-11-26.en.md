# Today's Highlights

The most-discussed story today was complexity theorist Scott Aaronson's wide-eyed dispatch about [AI rewriting math history](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379). Within a single week came proofs of VP versus VNP, an exact value of 9/4 for the matrix-multiplication exponent with matching bounds, and tighter Fourier-transform complexity bounds, which made even the Riemann Hypothesis proof feel like last week's news. If those names mean nothing to you, here is the gist: long-standing puzzles that resisted humans for decades are now falling in batches, each arriving with a machine-checkable certificate. On the same front page, though, OpenAI was getting roasted over a "billing fix" that users read as shrinking subscription quotas. Awe and suspicion ran side by side, with Google tightening agent access to inboxes and Europeans pushing a homegrown office suite. Start with the math storm, then follow how trust, rules, and daily life get rewritten alongside it.

## Science and Research

### AI keeps cracking math, and Aaronson can't hold back

[Scott Aaronson's blog](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=10379) records a week that kept him up at night: Anthropic and OpenAI each produced proofs of VP versus VNP, roughly a rigorous confirmation that two kinds of computational cost are fundamentally different. OpenAI then pinned the matrix-multiplication exponent at exactly 9/4, upper and lower bounds interlocking, and improved on the sixty-year-old Fast Fourier Transform bounds. The papers skipped press releases entirely and landed on GitHub with Lean machine-verification certificates attached. Aaronson argues AI is not picking off problems one by one but opening a whole new continent of mathematical truth, with a proof of P versus NP possibly not far off. He also cautions that some of the new algorithms could shake current cryptography, while declining to spread unverified rumors before seeing details.

Note: several readers praised the readability of this latest wave of AI papers, a big step up from the earlier "proof slop"; others pushed back on the supply chain behind Lean verifiers themselves. The thread's main split was whether quibbling over details could still overturn the proofs, with the prevailing view that dozens of long-open problems really are being settled. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50646108)

### A new attempt at quantum gravity amid the noise

A 54-page preprint, [An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34924), proposes a non-perturbative quantization of vacuum gravity: solve the constraints on intersecting light cones, build a Hilbert space, keep the state space of a macroscopic region finite-dimensional, and recover black-hole entropy plus its corrections in agreement with known results. In plain terms, it tries to make a quantum theory of gravity both mathematically consistent and able to reproduce the "standard answers". One author is a well-known figure in the black-hole information debate.

Note: top comments lamented that humanity's first credible non-perturbative quantum-gravity model couldn't even get a line of headline space, drowned out by the Riemann and P-versus-NP news; skeptics compared quantum-gravity theories to programming languages, with a new one monthly and none catching on. Other readers dug into technical objections page by page, while supporters called them fixable. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651863)

## Tech and Products

### Why a three-year-old Intel font is trending again

Intel's open-source monospace typeface [Intel One Mono](https://github.com/intel/intel-one-mono), released in 2023, resurfaced on the front page for its legibility. Low-vision and legally blind developers advised throughout the design, which focused on telling apart confusables like 0 and O or 1 and lowercase l, plus coding ligatures and full-format downloads, all free. For a general reader, this is simply what the letters and digits on your screen look like, thousands of times a day.

Note: comments reprised the classic font-holy-war, with one side saying decades of default fonts never caused them a misread, pure bikeshedding; the other side reciting war stories of misprinted labels and mangled URLs. One lawyer reader confessed to seeing no difference between fonts and was likened to a drinker of instant coffee, which seemed fair enough. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50653667)

### AI made tinkering with home servers fun again

Blogger Jan Lukas writes in [AI made self-hosting fun again](https://janlukas.blog/posts/ai-self-hosting) that after years of running home servers, his enthusiasm had faded, until AI coding assistants revived the hobby. Configurations that once demanded an evening of manual reading, from declarative system setups to custom boot images and automated update pipelines, now come together in half a day, plus handy bespoke tools for downloading invoices and e-papers. He also warns that every extra service is extra maintenance, and AI-generated code carries bugs and tech debt like any other: before building something just because you can, ask whether it is worth building.

Note: the comments turned into a show-and-tell of side projects, from procedurally generated endless runners to a family Scrabble board on the living-room TV. Several readers reached for a gardening metaphor: AI is fertilizer that grows weeds as fast as vegetables, so the job shifts from planting to pruning. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50660180)

### Reloading the page as a form of protest

The open-source extension [YouTube Ad Punisher](https://github.com/antonpictures/youtube-punisher) has a disarmingly simple idea: when an ad appears, save the playback position, reload the page, and YouTube seeks back to where you were, usually with the ad slot empty after a cycle or two. It also logs each ad's advertiser and duration for export as a table. The author frames it as zero-cost passive protest: if a million people did it, every reload would be a vote saying "I will not watch your ad." The project is MIT-licensed but needs manual installation, and any YouTube page-structure change can break it.

Note: the thread split fast, with one camp cheering a weapon of the weak against unbearable ad density and recommending pairing it with classic blockers; the other camp asked why heavy YouTube watchers won't just pay for membership, since creators need to eat too. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657530)

## Business and Platforms

### OpenAI's "billing fix" reads like a quota cut

OpenAI staffer Tibo [announced on X](https://twitter.com/thsottiaux/status/2125784147889393806) that a launch-time billing error had discounted GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol usage, and with the fix, the same work would hit plan limits sooner. As an apology, every paid tier gets one extra banked reset. The post drew millions of views and an outcry: users complained of steadily shrinking allowances and vague notification emails, plus a change that merges chat quota with coding quota, removing one of the subscription's last big draws.

Note: several readers compared notes on migrating between providers as the "more generous side" keeps flipping, arguing the balance tipped decisively after the newest models; others called the new models lazier and more evasive, and mused about going local. A competing theory held that OpenAI got profitability marching orders at the worst possible moment. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651117)

### Europe's Office suite gains a desktop app

Open-source cloud vendor Nextcloud shipped a [desktop edition of Nextcloud Office](https://nextcloud.com/blog/euro-office-comes-to-your-desktop-with-the-nextcloud-office-desktop-app/), completing its "Euro-Office" suite so documents and spreadsheets work offline too. Built by a consortium of European firms as a fork of OnlyOffice, the suite is framed as part of Europe's digital-sovereignty push and a direct Microsoft 365 rival. For ordinary users it means one more desktop Office choice that doesn't pipe files to American servers, though ecosystem and compatibility still trail.

Note: the thread fixated on the fork feud, with OnlyOffice accusing the fork of violating extra branding terms and the Free Software Foundation backing the forkers' reading that the license permits dropping such restrictions. Other readers questioned the sovereignty narrative or doubted that a consortium can build software without bureaucracy. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50654289)

### The trust problem with discount AI gateways

The membership gateway [Moat](https://commandcode.ai/moat) bills itself as an OpenRouter replacement: pay a membership fee, then call big-name models below list price through what it vaguely calls wholesale rates. For ordinary users, the question is whose hands your code, documents, and chat logs pass through.

Note: top comments converged on trust, arguing that handing source code and tool-call permissions to an obscure middleman is leaving the door open; others did the math and found the discounts mostly mirror upstream's own limited-time deals, doubting it is the same kind of product as OpenRouter at all. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50652184)

## Policy and Governance

### Google draws a line: agents must use the dedicated channel

Google made its eight Workspace MCP servers generally available with new terms: from 1 February 2027, any tool that "is or is driven by" an AI agent must reach [Gmail](https://techstackups.com/articles/google-workspace-ai-agents-mcp/), Drive, Calendar, and the rest through those channels, with quota throttling or suspension for going around them. Notably, the public Gmail channel can read mail and create drafts but sending, replying, and forwarding are "partner-only", and memory or vector indexes built from mailbox data face a 30-day retention cap. The article's author reads this as both a security design against prompt-injection mail abuse and a power grab over which agents get to do what.

Note: indie developers complained loudest, pointing out that a big vendor's official connector can send mail while a grassroots plugin may only draft it; others countered that a single gateway lets IT admins govern agent behavior in one place, with the real disagreement being who sets the rules. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50653477)

### The "recovery firm" that fed victims back to the ransomware gangs

Security reporter Brian Krebs reports that the CTO and co-founder of [RansomwareHelp](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2026/11/cto-of-ransomwarehelp-indicted-for-wire-fraud/), which advertised proprietary cryptographic tools with a 99 percent success rate, has been indicted for wire fraud. An FBI sting found the firm quietly buying decryptors from ransomware gangs while claiming it never paid ransoms, then marking up the price severalfold behind jargon about hundreds of forensic servers brute-forcing encryption. It is the Justice Department's third move against the ransomware-negotiation gray trade in two months.

Note: the thread's main split was legal rather than technical: if victims got their data back, is it fraud? Highly rated replies said the markup isn't the point, the fiction is, since customers came precisely to avoid funding criminals and may have tripped sanctions rules instead. Practitioners added that paying ransoms is flatly banned at many companies, making cutout intermediaries even riskier. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50657645)

## Society and Culture

### Eighty UI guides: a visible history of computing taste

Designer Marcin Wichary collected [eighty preserved interface style guides](https://aresluna.org/ui-style-guides/) spanning the 1980s to today, covering KDE, the PenPoint tablet system, and others, some scanned by hand, some mirrored for readability, all bundled as an offline archive. These documents dictated what buttons, menus, and dialogs should look like, the constitutions of software's appearance. For a general reader, it is a tangible history of computing aesthetics: why early software wore gray beveled buttons and everything today is flat.

Note: the breakout moment was a 1991 Amiga guide's opening note that English lacks a good gender-neutral third-person pronoun, so the book switches genders at random: "if you find yourself startled, maybe you needed to be." Readers relitigated language and gender around a thirty-year-old paragraph, while others mourned the lost clarity of the gray era. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50649834)

### The world-class architect the world just met

Works in Progress profiles [Kamil Khan Mumtaz](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-greatest-architect-youve-never-heard-of-is-in-pakistan/) of Lahore with photographs never before published outside Pakistan. Born in British India in 1939 and trained as a modernist in London, he returned home, spent time in prison for leftist activism, then turned at forty to reviving Punjabi building crafts, relearning lost brickwork and vaulting with hereditary artisans across homes, mosques, and a publisher's headquarters. The old walled city he helped save is now a World Heritage site. The piece asks: had global architecture media done one photoshoot in Lahore, would the world have known him decades ago?

Note: comments drifted into a cross-border travelogue, with one recollection of surprisingly clean Lahore streets promptly fact-checked against air-quality data and countered with seasonality arguments. Other readers reflected that greatness sometimes depends only on where the limelight falls. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50659841)

### The "banned book" that spread by photocopier for twenty years

The F/OSS Comics series retells a legend in panels: in 1976, John Lions of the University of New South Wales annotated the Unix Version 6 kernel line by line for his operating-systems course, until AT&T's 1979 license clampdown banned teaching from source, and [Lions' Commentary](https://fosscomics.com/posts/17.%20Lions'%20Commentary%20on%20Unix/) spread through universities by photocopier for two decades as hacker samizdat. The comic turns dry licensing history into an underground-distribution thriller while explaining why OS courses insist on reading real code.

Note: veteran programmers waxed nostalgic, with several arguing the book and its Minix successor put Unix-lineage systems in every classroom, or history might have gone commercial; others doubted the AI era could still produce such line-by-line textbooks, though some countered that AI is exactly good at annotation and only lacks exacting humans. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50651719)
