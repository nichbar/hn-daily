# Today's Highlights

The biggest story on Hacker News today belongs to mathematics: a [draft paper by Levent Alpöge claiming a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) drew the day's largest discussion, and a Stanford professor posted a second, separate claim almost simultaneously. Around that double headline, the rest of the day orbited the same question from different angles: what happens as AI starts doing the hardest math, designing chips, reading phone screens, and writing student essays.

## Science and Research

### Two claimed proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis in one day

[Levent Alpöge's draft](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) targets the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the conjecture about the deep pattern of prime numbers and one of the seven Millennium Prize problems. The draft is tied to a proof of finite-time blow-up for the three-dimensional unforced Euler equations, and discussion on the thread holds that both efforts involved AI heavily. Terence Tao followed the developments on his blog with a status summary, though peer review and formal verification are still underway. The thread swings between witnessing-history excitement and scholarly caution: several readers frame it as the leap from Olympiad gold to a Millennium problem, while mathematically trained commenters urge waiting for verification before celebrating. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50574968).

### The day's second Riemann claim

Almost in parallel, [Ravi Vakil's hurried announcement](https://math216.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/hurried-announcement-alessandro-masullo-grh/) presented another proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis by Alessandro Maria Masullo. According to Vakil, Brian Conrad spent months reviewing it with extensive feedback, an all-human effort originally meant to be polished for a year before release. Dramatically, an update about eight hours later reported that several mathematicians, assisted by different AI models, had found problems in the proof, and Vakil himself declined to vouch for its strategy. Commenters fixated on the coincidence and on verification: whether the two proofs are independent, whether review pipelines get cross-contaminated by AI, and whether publishing unfinished drafts early helps or hurts. Several readers observed that AI-assisted checking is rewriting the tempo of mathematical refereeing. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50579568).

### A model that edits its own weights

[An arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21885) introduces self-rewriting: an open 27B model, given its own weights, graded answers, and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its failures and rewrites its weights using only forward passes, gaining about 6.6 percentage points of accuracy on unseen problems and improving across eight consecutive rounds. The paper also claims the model can repair a near-broken copy of itself back to over 80 percent of original accuracy, while smaller models either refuse to edit or break themselves, suggesting the ability emerges with scale. The thread quickly cooled the hype: readers noted the main fix was shortening reasoning traces to dodge token limits, an obvious-flaw repair rather than open-ended self-improvement, and cited parallel weight-space programming work as supporting only the narrower reading. Defenders countered that read-write access to weights is itself a first. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577265).

## Tech and Products

### A frontier-class model for your laptop

[Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/), aimed at running locally on a laptop. It uses a looped transformer that runs its middle layers three times per token, buying deeper reasoning at a smaller parameter count. The company reports 26.4B total parameters with 3.4B active, leading every open-weights model under 100B parameters on coding benchmarks such as DeepSWE, with low-bit quantization training that keeps local deployment friendly. Commenters are eager for independent tests: several relayed the team's forum notes that looping helps coding tasks and survives quantization well, while skeptics warned that the official charts rest on theoretical throughput assumptions that real usage may not match. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260).

### A CPU discovered and designed by AI

[Architect Labs introduced Frostbite](https://architectlabs.com/blog/frostbite), billed as a general-purpose CPU with a from-scratch instruction set, discovered, designed, and verified end to end by AI. The team credits interaction-net properties, locality, explicit dependencies, and single ownership, for roughly a 4.59x performance-per-watt multiple over a modern dual-issue in-order superscalar core across 25 workloads. By the company's account, AI handled the full stack from compiler and architecture down to RTL, verification, and final layout. Engineers in the thread split between doubt and curiosity: many asked whether the baseline is strong enough and the workloads reproducible, while others focused on the method, debating whether joint software-hardware optimization by AI could open design spaces humans never reach. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572503).

### Google's new model tops a third-party engineering ranking

The third-party [Models Engineering Index updated its leaderboard](https://git-aarvin.github.io/mei/) with Gemini 4 Neon on top. The index blends sixteen coding benchmarks, normalized for difficulty and missing results onto one scale, alongside relative cost and token use. The most interesting comments came from people actually using the model: one reader called Neon a current favorite, on par with top rivals for coding, unusually clean at editing documents, and even opinionated enough to criticize bloated instructions. Others pushed back that Google makes its models hard to try without heavy spending, so the leaderboard looks better than the access feels. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50568130).

## Policy and Governance

### Signal moves to block phone AI from reading chats

[Signal president Meredith Whittaker announced](https://signal.org/blog/protecting-your-privacy/) that the app will restrict operating-system AI assistants from capturing Signal content, extending last year's screenshot blocking against Windows Recall to detectable on-screen reading by AI on iOS and Android. Signal argues that with assistants baked deep into the OS by Google and Apple, the platform stops being neutral ground and becomes an active party that can lift whatever is rendered on screen, an existential challenge to end-to-end encryption. The thread's main technical objection is the boundary problem: several readers noted that anything rendered enters the UI tree, which encryption cannot protect, and that a chat partner's AI leaks your messages even if yours does not. Many praised the defensive move while conceding the real rules depend on whether Apple and Google ship proper opt-outs for sensitive apps. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572348).

### Why iPhones still have no real third-party browsers

[Mozilla explained](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/internet-policy/2026/11/20/apple-ios-browser-engine-rules/) why iOS still has no genuine alternative browser engine: Apple requires every iOS browser to build on its own WebKit, while much of Firefox lives in its Gecko engine and cannot be ported over. Mozilla says the EU's Digital Markets Act, which forced Apple to offer default-browser choice in 2024, doubled Firefox's daily iPhone use, yet the engine restriction persists and Apple's compliance path never truly opened it. Commenters asked the blunt question of what Apple gains: some cited the UK regulator's finding that closedness pays, while the technical camp pointed to just-in-time compilation as the crux, arguing openness would expose system capabilities and shake the App Store model. Many held up macOS as the counterexample: if the desktop can allow engine choice, so should the phone. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50575644).

### Google workers oppose punishing schools that switch off AI

[Mother Jones reported](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/11/google-alphabet-union-school-ai-protest-gemini-workers-petition/) that hundreds of Google workers petitioned management to retract a policy cutting off services to schools that disable its Gemini AI tools. The workers, organized through the Alphabet union, argue leadership mistakes principled refusal for missing the vision, while schools need a no-AI option, just as Google pushes search toward AI-first and Chromebooks deeper into classrooms. The thread split on what the demand really costs: some readers framed it as asking Google to maintain two product lines indefinitely, while others countered that turning off generative summaries and keeping classic search is hardly beyond a company of that size. Underneath sits the deeper disagreement over whether AI should be on by default. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50577901).

## Business and Platforms

### When the repairable laptop brand fails at support

[OpenBSD developer Gilles Chehade wrote](https://poolp.org/posts/2026-11-20/ill-never-buy-framework-hardware-again/) that he will never buy Framework hardware again over a poor support experience. The post itself has since been trimmed to a call for Framework to fix support and deliver a working laptop worthy of the price. After the story blew up, Framework's founder replied on HN, conceding that longevity must include a smooth support experience alongside repairable hardware, and describing shorter replacement-part flows and a tighter returns-to-engineering loop. The thread divided in two: prospective buyers worried that technical customers fear being treated as novices above all, while sympathizers noted support is expensive and hard to staff. Many credited the founder for showing up in person, with the caveat that follow-through is what counts. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50576855).

## Society and Culture

### If you cannot explain it simply, do you understand it

[A 2017 essay](https://kottke.org/17/06/if-you-cant-explain-something-in-simple-terms-you-dont-understand-it) revisits the line often attributed to Feynman: real understanding shows in simple explanation. Starting from Feynman's lost lecture on planetary motion, it recalls his gift for making hard ideas plain, and its HN revival, amid AI-written homework, turned into a debate about what understanding actually requires. The top pushback was the most telling: using compactness in analysis as an example, readers argued the definition can be stated plainly while genuine grasp still demands working problems by hand over time, so not every failure to explain is the explainer's fault. The thread's rough consensus held that explanatory power tests understanding without exhausting it. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572355).

### One cruise line out-polluting all of Europe's cars

[A 2019 report](https://ecohustler.com/article/one-corporation-to-pollute-them-all/) citing Transport and Environment found the world's largest cruise operator, Carnival, emitting nearly ten times the sulphur oxide around European coasts as all 260 million European cars combined in 2017. Burning cheap bunker fuel, the ships endanger health and acidify land and sea, with Spain, Italy, Greece, Barcelona, and Venice among the hardest hit. Comments quickly moved past cruise ships to the thornier science: several readers noted that stripping sulphur from shipping fuels thinned ocean cloud cover and may have accelerated warming in the 2020s, so cleanup carries side effects. The shared takeaway was that the pollution numbers are staggering, yet climate management has no simple off switch. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572116).

### Praise that stings: I can tell you wrote this yourself

[A student's account](https://em-online.bearblog.dev/i-can-tell-you-wrote-this-yourself/) describes a supervisor meeting where the first reaction to a midterm report was recognition: the supervisor could tell it was self-written, because so many submissions now arrive machine-made. The author writes that coursework defaults to encouraging, even expecting, AI use, with opting out a disadvantage, and the hardest class meant eight-hour days of prompting in a windowless lab. The post throws the question back: when the process of writing is the learning, what is a finished product that skipped the process worth. The top comment framed knowledge work as industrialization: cheap shoes are a triumph, but for writing the process is the point, and documents that bypass thinking are nearly valueless. Unusually for HN, the tone stayed sympathetic throughout. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50571189).
