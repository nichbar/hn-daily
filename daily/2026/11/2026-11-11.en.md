# Today's Highlights

Hacker News opened today with an obituary. Guido van Rossum announced that [Tim Peters](https://discuss.python.org/t/in-memoriam-tim-peters-1952-2026/110028), author of the Zen of Python and inventor of Timsort, died earlier this year. Anyone who has ever typed `import this` has read his nineteen aphorisms, and the thread became a global wall of remembrance, with many wondering whether that philosophy of simplicity survives in the age of agents writing code.

Beyond the farewell, the day was about boundaries: how far AI reaches, and where the world pushes back. Off-the-shelf agents sifted public radar data and pinned down a Maya site in under 72 hours, while United Airlines banned AI assistants from booking flights and a reporter grilled eleven TV makers on what their sets watch. Meanwhile the cloud and the device renegotiated their split, with AWS shipping an official local emulator and Apple reportedly turning the Mac into a private AI server.

## Society and Culture

### Tim Peters, author of the Zen of Python, has died

Guido van Rossum announced on the official Python forum that core developer [Tim Peters](https://discuss.python.org/t/in-memoriam-tim-peters-1952-2026/110028) died on July 15. Peters co-shaped early Python: he wrote the Zen of Python, designed [Timsort](https://discuss.python.org/t/in-memoriam-tim-peters-1952-2026/110028) in 2002, later adopted by Java and Android, and once persuaded van Rossum to allow mixed integer-float arithmetic. On the mailing lists he answered countless questions with unfailing patience, earning the nickname "Timbot" and signing off with his trademark `<wink>` humor.

The thread's consensus was that "Simple is better than complex" is more than a slogan; for many readers it is the baseline of how they write and design. Several commenters noted that an elegant algorithm like Timsort quietly running for decades is the purest evidence of Peters's style, while others hoped a new generation keeps that simplicity alive as agents take over more coding. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50450488).

### A false alarm nearly drowned a Wall Street datacenter

A former Jane Street systems engineer [recounts a night of terror](https://michael.bacarella.com/2026/11/10/false-smoke-alarm-jane-street-datacenter/): construction dust tripped the smoke alarm, which by design cut the cooling and pressurized the sprinkler pipes, waiting only for enough heat to spray. With a room full of servers and no cooling, the temperature climbed fast. The author sprinted back to the office and pulled every power cord cabinet by cabinet until the thermometer stalled near 143.6°F. The piece also recalls the firm's stubborn culture of keeping the machine room across the hall so someone could always kill the power by hand.

Readers answered with their own datacenter near-misses, from clogged generator filters to accidental fire-suppression discharges. The thread's main takeaway was that even the most automated operation still needs a pair of legs that can run there, and that kind of keep-it-reachable stubbornness is rare today. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50455394).

### "More goat sacrifices" before grand plans

An essay titled [We need more goat sacrifices](https://www.experimental-history.com/p/we-need-more-goat-sacrifices) uses a joke to make a serious case: before any grand social policy, stage a ritual that induces humility, because so many well-meant interventions do nothing. The author invokes sociologist Peter Rossi's "Iron Law of Evaluation" and cites cases such as tuition-free medical school leaving student composition unchanged and large cash-transfer trials returning null effects, blaming an "illusion of control" in which good intentions replace goal-setting and measurement.

The comments hosted one of the day's fiercest debates. Several readers pushed back with the plain-packaging example, arguing that falling overall smoking rates mean side effects are not failure. Others endorsed the core critique that many institutions spend money without ever defining success. The thread's main split was whether admitting limits should mean acting more cautiously or measuring more seriously. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50452652).

## Science and Research

### AI agents rediscover a Maya site in 72 hours

While traveling in Peru, the author had a thought: set AI agents loose on NASA's public [lidar data](https://pietromiotti.com/latest/maya-site) to hunt for Maya ruins in Mexico. Forty-eight agents paged through terrain imagery across some 1,200 square kilometers of jungle, flagging pyramid-, platform-, and plaza-shaped anomalies, and converged on a site missing from Mexico's official register, drawing a formal reply from the authorities. In an update the author concedes the site had been recorded in a 2025 paper, making it a rediscovery, but the timeline from idle thought to government response still measured under 72 hours.

Top comments argued that this division of labor, agents scanning images and humans judging, could generalize to many neglected fields. Several readers cautioned, though, that absence from a register is not absence of knowledge, and fieldwork remains the real confirmation; for now, AI is a lead generator, not an arbiter. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50452455).

### One equation reproduces AI's eight-hour "brain surgery"

After MIT CSAIL showed that a large model can read small-network weights and even write game-playing weights from scratch, a [reproduction](https://nathan.rs/posts/weight-space-lp/) finds that one striking edit, which lifted a small model's math accuracy from 7.9% to 48.1%, amounts to solving a linear program. Two solves of about a minute each reproduced what the model found through eight hours of trial and error, touching one-eighth as many weights. Understanding a model's internals, the piece argues, can beat brute-force search by a wide margin.

Researchers in the thread were excited that interpretability is starting to pay practical dividends, turning black-box tuning into something computable. Others pushed back that this success leaned on a single well-oriented failure mode, "thinking loops," and it is unclear how far the trick extends to more diffuse capability gaps. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50452655).

## Tech and Products

### AWS ships an official local cloud emulator

Amazon Web Services open-sourced [AWS Emulator](https://github.com/aws/aws-emulator), a program that serves cloud API endpoints on a developer's own machine. No cloud account is needed: point the CLI or SDK at the emulator and test as usual, with requests staying local and no bill attached. It supports server mode and embedded Rust-test mode, plus an AI-agent skill for one-line setup. The docs stress it is for building and testing only, not for production or security evaluation.

The thread welcomed an official emulator as more trustworthy than third-party imitations, especially for offline work and saving money. The shared caveat in top comments was coverage: which services behave faithfully, and how wide the gap to the real cloud is, will decide whether it prevents surprises or merely postpones them. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50456631).

### Locking SSH keys inside the Mac's Secure Enclave

A hands-on guide explains how to keep [SSH login keys](https://gatezh.com/posts/macos-secure-enclave-ssh-keys/) inside the Mac's Secure Enclave chip: keys are generated on-chip and can never be exported, and every use requires a Touch ID confirmation, so even a compromised machine gives up nothing. The post compares the native approach with tools like Secretive and walks through generation, configuration, backup, and recovery. In an update the author says he has since switched to Secretive for its smoother prompts and promises a follow-up.

Many readers said this is overdue, calling hardware-bound keys an effective defense against theft and phishing. Others flagged the familiar pain of cross-device sync and migration after a hardware swap, with several arguing usability remains the main barrier to adoption. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50449748).

### A hyper-realistic sandbox that lives in your browser

The [hyper-realistic sand simulator](https://av.github.io/safe-sand/) built with WebGL2 and JavaScript runs on phones, tablets, and desktops with nothing to install. Push grains with a finger, lay the phone flat and tilt it to make sand slide, or shake to scatter it. The author squeezed physics simulation and rendering into smooth in-browser performance, so the page itself is the toy.

The comments read like playtest notes: praise for tactile realism, tips to lay the phone flat with the screen up, and the discovery that turning it over pours the sand out. Developers in the thread asked about the particle simulation and optimization behind it, while several readers reported slideshows on older phones and some browsers. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50453288).

## Business and Platforms

### Apple's Harbor app would turn the Mac into a private AI server

Citing sources, 9to5Mac reports Apple is building a new Mac app, codenamed [Harbor](https://9to5mac.com/2026/11/10/apple-has-new-mac-app-coming-to-make-running-local-ai-models-easy/), that turns the machine into a private on-device inference server so users can run models locally instead of sending data to the cloud. The app is said to shine brightest on next-generation chips, fitting Apple's long-standing privacy story that sensitive computation stays on the device.

The thread split in two. One camp sees privacy and offline availability as a genuine ecosystem edge; the other doubts mainstream laptops have the memory and battery headroom for serious models and expects most people to keep using cloud services. Underneath the split sits one shared question: has the price-performance crossover for on-device models actually arrived. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50446520).

### Google's Decisions API turns AI from writing into judging

Google launched the [Gemini Decisions API](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemini-decisions-api/), powered by a new model, Gemini Decision 1, built for choosing rather than composing. One request can put up to 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice, or rubric questions to mixed text, image, audio, video, and PDF input, and the model grades the options in a single forward pass, returning probabilities with confidence values at roughly 150 to 250 milliseconds end to end, billed on input only. Google claims benchmark leads with lower calibration error at about one-fifth the cost of its flagship light model, targeting tool selection, moderation, and model routing.

Several readers liked the idea of judgment-only endpoints cutting cost and uncertainty in agent systems. Others asked for independent benchmarks rather than vendor numbers, and questioned how much confidence values actually help real-world fallback and review pipelines. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50456791).

## Policy and Governance

### United Airlines says no to AI booking agents

United Airlines announced it will bar third-party personal [AI assistants](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/united-airlines-bans-ai-agents-booking-flights/) from booking tickets or checking travelers in, citing security risks and possible federal-rule conflicts. Handing account credentials to an agent risks leaks, mistaken purchases, and burned miles, the airline said, and baggage attestation at check-in must be done by the traveler in person. Enforcement will run through its own platforms, agencies, and aggregators, while Southwest takes the opposite bet by letting ChatGPT users browse its inventory.

Commenters divided between support, on the grounds that payment credentials should never go to an agent, and suspicion that an airline protecting its booking funnel found a convenient excuse in federal rules. A recurring question was which carriers follow with bans and which embrace the agent channel. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50458900).

### A reporter asked eleven TV makers seventeen privacy questions

After LG faced accusations of spying through its sets, a Verge reporter sent [seventeen privacy questions](https://www.theverge.com/tech/1012372/tv-privacy-acr-questions) to eleven companies, including Amazon, Apple, Google, Hisense, LG, Samsung, Sony, TCL, Roku, Panasonic, and Walmart's Vizio, covering automatic content recognition (technology that identifies what is on screen for ads and recommendations), microphone listening, and opt-outs. Apple and Google said their own systems do not log viewing this way; LG said it uses audio fingerprints, off by default; Hisense ignored five contact attempts. The piece also notes a Northeastern study finding some sets still transmitting after opt-out.

The comment most echoed was that questionnaires capture official claims while only packet captures capture behavior. Many readers said they simply keep their TVs offline behind a set-top box and firewall, while others argued that on-by-default tracking users must individually disable is unfair by design. Discussion: [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50453474).
